### Station 1: The problem by hand

By the early 1960s you could read a protein as a string of letters. Myoglobin
and the two hemoglobin chains had been sequenced, and Kendrew and Perutz had
shown that they fold into almost the same shape. So were they related? If they
were, the sequences should show it too. To find out, you line one sequence up
against the other and count the places where they agree.

That sounds easy. This station is about why it isn't.

The scoring is the simplest rule Needleman and Wunsch used: a column scores 1
when both letters are the same amino acid, and 0 otherwise. Each letter is one
amino acid, in the one-letter code that Margaret Dayhoff's group adopted for
their Atlas of protein sequences.

<PaperNote cite="Strasser (2010), p. 639">
  "they adopted a one-letter notation for amino acids, instead of the usual
  three letter code, in order to save computer memory and to make alignments
  more readable on fixed-space printers."
</PaperNote>

#### Sliding

The obvious first move is to slide one sequence along the other. Every position
gives each letter a new partner, and the score counts the columns that agree.
Each slide you try leaves a bar in the chart underneath, so you can see which

#### One slide isn't enough

Proteins gain and lose residues as they evolve. So when one sequence has an
extra stretch in the middle, the part before it lines up at one slide and the
part after it lines up at another, and no single slide gets both. In this pair
the start already agrees. Can you get the end to agree too?

<StripPair
  {...PUZZLES.twoSlides}
  solved="K and T are in the top sequence and not the bottom one. Everything before the gap keeps one pairing and everything after it takes another."
/>

What you've made is a gap. Drag a letter right and it moves, along with
everything after it, while everything before it stays put. A gap marks an
insertion in one sequence or a deletion in the other, and from the sequences
alone you can't tell which. A slide is the same move made on the first letter,
so a slide is just a gap at the start of a row.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 443">
  "Allowance for gaps (Braunitzer, 1965) greatly multiplies the number of
  comparisons that can be made but introduces unnecessary and partial
  comparisons."
</PaperNote>

#### Gaps for free

So far gaps cost nothing, and that's a problem. These two sequences are random
and have nothing to do with each other. The best slide scores 3. See if you can
reach 6 with gaps.

<StripPair
  {...PUZZLES.freeCheat}
  solved="Free gaps doubled the score of two unrelated sequences."
/>

You can. With free gaps you can pair up almost any letters you like, so a high
score stops meaning anything. Real proteins are no different. With free gaps
Needleman and Wunsch matched 63 residues of β-hemoglobin and myoglobin, but it
took at least 35 gaps, and ten shuffled versions of β-hemoglobin still averaged
55.6 matches (Table 1, p. 449). Gibbs and McIntyre saw the same danger in
claims that proteins repeat their own sequences:

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 5">
  "few of the claimed repetitions have been tested statistitically [sic], and
  many rely on the addition of gaps to parts of the sequence, and this has
  inherent dangers."
</PaperNote>

#### Charging for gaps

The fix is to make gaps cost something. Needleman and Wunsch subtract a fixed
penalty for every gap, however long it is, so a gap has to win back more
matches than it costs. Gaps hanging off the ends of a row are free, because an
alignment can start and stop anywhere.

<PaperNote
  cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 444"
  differs="The paper never says end gaps are free. It follows from where their pathways may start and stop (pp. 444, 446)."
>
  "A penalty factor, a number subtracted for every gap made, may be assessed as
  a barrier to allowing the gap… No gap would be allowed in the operation unless
  the benefit from allowing that gap would exceed the barrier."
</PaperNote>

Here's the random pair again, at a cost of 1 per gap. Try the gaps that worked
before.

  {...PUZZLES.costCheat}
  solved="The extra gaps don't pay anymore, and the score falls back to what a slide gives."
/>

Now the related pair from before, at the same cost. Its gap marks something
real, so it should still pay.

/>

That's the point of the penalty. It lets real gaps through and keeps the
opportunistic ones out.

#### Too many to check

On fragments this short you can find the best alignment by fiddling. On whole
proteins you can't, because the number of possible alignments explodes. Drag
the lengths up and watch.

<AlignmentCount />

The two numbers count slightly different things. Look at these two alignments.
Between L and H, the top row has a K with no partner and the bottom row has a T
with no partner, and the two gaps can go in either order.

<GapOrder />

Are they different alignments? They pair exactly the same residues, so the
smaller count says no and counts them once. The larger count says yes. Sean
Eddy's primer on dynamic programming uses the smaller one. Either way,
β-hemoglobin against myoglobin has somewhere between 10<sup>88</sup> and
10<sup>113</sup> alignments. Nobody could check them all by hand, and no
computer of the 1960s could either.

<PaperNote cite="Eddy (2004), p. 909">
  "Why do we need a fancy algorithm? Can't we just score all possible
  alignments and pick the best one? This isn't practical".
</PaperNote>

So before 1970, alignment came down to judgment twice over. People lined
sequences up by eye, and then decided by eye whether the result meant
anything.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 443">
  "Because the method of visual comparison is tedious and because the
  determination of the significance of a given result usually is left to
  intuitive rationalization, computer-based statistical approaches have been
  proposed (Fitch, 1966; Needleman & Blair, 1969)."
</PaperNote>

#### What station 1 leaves open

That leaves two questions. How do you find the best alignment without trying
them all? And how do you tell a real similarity from chance? Needleman and
Wunsch answered the first with their array, in stations 4 and 5, and the second
with the shuffle test, in station 7. But four years earlier Walter Fitch had
found a way around the first question altogether.

### Station 2: Fitch's windows

Fitch's way around it was to never place a gap. At the University of Wisconsin
in 1965, he cut both sequences into overlapping stretches of 30 residues, which
this page calls windows, and compared every window of one with every window of
the other. Nothing slides inside a window. A deletion doesn't vanish, though:
before it the windows line up at one offset, and after it at another.

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 9">
  "a deletion in the middle of one protein would throw the homologous relation
  out of register at that point"
</PaperNote>

#### Scoring by codons

Fitch changed the score too. Station 1 only asked whether two amino acids were
the same. Fitch asked how close they are: how many DNA bases would have to
change to turn one into the other. Most amino acids have several codons, so
the answer is the fewest changes between any codon of one and any codon of the
other. It's 0 for the same amino acid and never more than 3. So now the score
runs the other way, and lower is closer.

The table is Fitch's own (Table 1, p. 10). He built it from the genetic code as
Trupin, Nirenberg and colleagues had read it in 1965, which wasn't finished
yet, and 11 of the 190 pairs differ from today's code. Point at a pair to see
its codons, or switch to today's code to see which pairs changed.

<CodonDistance />

K and E are one base apart: AAA becomes GAA. Most pairs take one or two
changes, and only a few take all three. The rest of this station uses Fitch's
table, the way he did.

#### One window against another

Add up a window's 30 column scores and you get its score, which Fitch called
MR, the minimum mutations required. By his calculation two unrelated windows of
α- and β-hemoglobin average 43.5, about 1.45 per pair (p. 12). He drew the line
at 34. A window at or under 34 counts as a sign of common ancestry, because
chance gets that low less than 1% of the time.

Try a few windows. The register shift, α's start minus β's, says how far apart
the two windows sit.

<WindowPair />

Start both chains at residue 1 and you get 35, just over the line. Move β along
by one, to α 2 against β 3, and it drops to 26. That's a shift of −1, because β
has an extra residue at its start. α 81 against β 86, at a shift of −5, scores
16, the lowest anywhere. α 60 against β 20 scores 42, about what chance gives.

Look at the light green rungs. They're pairs one base change apart, and each
adds just 1 to the score, where an unrelated pair adds about 1.5. So near
misses pull a related window down along with the identical pairs, and that's
why Fitch's windows pick up more than the eye does.

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 15">
  "Finding the homologous regions is not simply the result of observing those
  identical amino acid pairings that can be seen visually."
</PaperNote>

#### Every window against every window

α has 112 windows of 30 and β has 117, so there are 13,104 pairs to score, and
Fitch's program scored them all. Here each pair is a square, with α's window
start down the side and β's along the top. Moving down a diagonal keeps the
register shift fixed. The squares at or under 34 are green, and most of them
sit on three diagonals.

<WindowMap />

Those three diagonals, at shifts of −1, +1 and −5, are the three Fitch found.
Read along β and the best diagonal changes twice. Each change means residues
were gained or lost somewhere in between. Here's how Fitch read it:

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 14">
  "The register shift of − 1 occurs because the β-chain has an extra amino
  acid at the beginning. A deletion of two β-chain amino acids between 20 and
  21 (Ingram, 1963) now gives the α-chain the extra amino acid and the
  register shift becomes + 1. A subsequent deletion of six amino acids in the
  α-chain produces a net shift of − 5."
</PaperNote>

The windows tell you a gap is there and roughly where, but not exactly where.
A window that straddles the gap is half in one register and half in the
other, so it scores badly in both. A second program in the same paper pinned
the β gap down:

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 15">
  "the β-chain deletion should really be between amino acids 18 and 19 so that
  asparagine (β) will coincide with histidine (α)."
</PaperNote>

<LabNote title="Against the paper">
  With his table and today's sequences, 172 windows score 34 or less, against
  his 179. The three main shifts hold 81, 38 and 11 windows against his 81, 38
  and 10, starting at β 37–117, 1–38 and 2–12 against his 37–117, 1–38 and
  2–11 (Table 2, p. 14). Two of his chance shifts come out identical too: +9 at
  β 62–70 and +61 at β 43–51.
</LabNote>

#### Is it more than chance?

How low would the windows score if α and β weren't related at all? Fitch
didn't shuffle sequences to find out. He worked it out from composition: draw
each pair at random from the amino acids of α and β, in the proportions they
occur, and add up 30 of them (p. 12). The dashed line is that calculation, and
the bars are all 13,104 real windows. This is his Fig. 1. Most windows follow
the line, but the low end doesn't.

<ScoreHistogram />

Zoom in on the low scores. Chance predicts about 93 windows at or under 34,
and 172 turn up.

Even that undersells it. Away from the three diagonals only 42 windows reach
the cutoff, where the calculation predicts about 91, so the calculation is
generous. Fitch saw the same thing when he compared human cytochrome c with
α-hemoglobin as a control: 37 low windows where 54 were expected. He put it
down to the windows not being independent of one another (p. 13).

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 13">
  "Thus, a statement that the probability of an MR value is ≤ 0·01
  over-estimates the likelihood of the chance occurrence."
</PaperNote>

So the count isn't what settles it. What settles it is where the low scores
sit, in long runs down a few diagonals.

<PaperNote cite="Fitch (1966), p. 14">
  "one does not expect to see the low MR values continue over a considerable
  portion of the two sequences if these values are the result of random
  events."
</PaperNote>

#### What station 2 leaves open

Fitch never searched for gaps, so station 1's explosion never came up, and
13,104 comparisons were enough. What you get back is a register shift for each
stretch, though, not an alignment, and a window of 30 blurs every gap it
crosses. Gibbs and McIntyre would drop the windows and look at every residue.
Needleman and Wunsch would put the gaps back and find the best place for them.

### Station 3: Gibbs and McIntyre's diagram

In 1970 Adrian Gibbs, of the microbiology department at the Australian National
University, and George McIntyre, a statistician at CSIRO, published the
simplest method of the three. Write one sequence down the left side of a grid
and the other along the top. Put a dot in every cell where the row and the
column hold the same amino acid. That's it. They called it the diagram. If two
sequences are related, their dots line up into a line running down and to the
right.

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 1">
  "it is basically simple (can be done by hand if necessary), and shows
  directly all the possible similarities between the sequences."
</PaperNote>

They kept station 1's scoring, too: a dot for the same amino acid and nothing
for anything else. They didn't trust Fitch's codons.

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 4">
  "we doubt the value of using the 'minimum mutational distance' measure used
  by Fitch [2,10] because of the great number of untested and untestable
  assumptions in this particular measure of similarity."
</PaperNote>

Real sequences add noise. In the globin fragment from station 1, plenty of

#### Cytochrome c

Their first case was cytochrome c, which by then had been sequenced from
enough species for them to compare 25 cytochromes. Here's human cytochrome c
against three of them. Monkey differs from human at one residue, so its line runs corner to corner,
broken once. Tuna differs at about a fifth of its residues, and the line breaks
up but stays on one diagonal. The bacterium *Rhodospirillum rubrum* is a
different story.

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 1">
  "the line of matches between the cytochromes of man and R. rubrum is, at
  different parts of the sequences, on three different diagonals, indicating
  that parts of one sequence are not present in the other."
</PaperNote>

The longest run there, five in a row, pairs human 95–99 with *R. rubrum*
105–109, ten diagonals off the main one. You can barely see it among the chance
dots. Something else shows up in all three, though: the same horizontal bands.
Lysine and glycine are common in human cytochrome c, so their rows fill up with
dots whatever they're compared against.

The monkey and tuna diagrams also have short lines parallel to the main one,
near the top left. The start of cytochrome c repeats itself:

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), pp. 1, 3">
  "the repetition in the N-terminal part (residues 3–8 and 20–25) of the
  sequence Val-Glu-Lys(or Asn)-Gly-Gly(or Lys)-Lys."
</PaperNote>

#### Counting instead of looking

Eyes are easy to fool, so Gibbs and McIntyre counted two things. They counted
the unbroken runs of dots along the diagonals, and they added up the dots on
each diagonal. Then they compared both with what chance would give (p. 1).
Like Fitch, they worked chance out from composition instead of shuffling. The
chance of a dot in any cell is just the number of matching pairs of amino
acids divided by the number of cells (p. 10).

Both counts are under the diagrams above. Monkey has two very long runs, where
chance expects none at all, and *R. rubrum*'s runs look like chance. Monkey's
main diagonal holds 103 dots and *R. rubrum*'s 21, against about 8 by chance.

<LabNote title="Against the paper">
  Human against monkey has 680 lone dots, 36 runs of two and 2 each of three
  and four; human against tuna has 636, 44, 4, 3, 3 and 1. Both are exactly
  their Table 1. *R. rubrum* gives 725, 46, 6, 1, 1 against their 729, 47, 6,
  1, 1. The main diagonals hold 103, 83 and 21, exactly their Table 2. For two
  random sequences with human cytochrome c's composition, chance gives 738
  lone dots, 58 runs of two and 8.4 on the main diagonal, as theirs. They count
  runs one shorter than this page does, so a lone dot is their run of 0
  (p. 10).
</LabNote>

#### Faint lines

Close relatives draw a clear line. Distant ones draw a faint one, and gaps
break it across neighboring diagonals. For the two chains of immunoglobulin,
where the lines are hard to see, Gibbs and McIntyre added each diagonal to its
four neighbors and plotted the running sums (Fig. 6B, p. 7). A line broken
across several diagonals then adds up in one place.

β-hemoglobin against myoglobin is a faint case too. Can you see the line?
Compare it with shuffles of β. A shuffle keeps every amino acid and only loses
the order, so it has exactly as many dots as the real pair, 1,533. What it
loses is the line.

<GlobinDiagram />

The dots alone can't tell a relative from a shuffle. The diagonals can. The
real pair puts 30 dots on one diagonal, where ten shuffles get to 18 at most,
and summed over five diagonals it peaks at 81 against at most 67. Here one
diagonal is already enough. Summing earns its keep when gaps have split a line,
as they did in the immunoglobulin chains.

Counting can even find what the eye can't. Their diagram of chicken lysozyme
against bovine α-lactalbumin (Fig. 4) shows no line at all, and yet:

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 5">
  "a runs index of 0.139, and a diagonals index of 0.032 with a probability of
  0.00025 of occurring by chance."
</PaperNote>

#### What station 3 leaves open

The diagram shows every similarity at once, and it doesn't choose between
them. To get an alignment you have to pick one path through it: which diagonals
to follow, where to jump, and whether each jump is worth it. Picking that path
by hand is station 1's problem all over again. Needleman and Wunsch's array, in
station 4, picks it for you.

### Station 4: Needleman and Wunsch's array

Station 1 ended on a problem. β-hemoglobin and myoglobin have more possible
alignments than anyone could ever check, so how do you find the best one?
Saul Needleman and Christian Wunsch, at Northwestern University and the VA
Research Hospital in Chicago, found a way that never looks at most of them.
Their paper reached the Journal of Molecular Biology in July 1969.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 443">
  "While the interruptions give rise to a very large number of comparisons,
  the method efficiently excludes from consideration those comparisons that
  cannot contribute to the maximum match."
</PaperNote>

Their array starts out as Gibbs and McIntyre's diagram: one protein across the
top, the other down the side, and a 1 in every cell where the two amino acids
are the same. An alignment is a pathway through it. Every step goes down and
to the right. One index moves by exactly one and the other can move by more,
so a step straight down the diagonal pairs the next two residues, and a longer
step skips residues in one sequence. That's a gap. A pathway scores the 1s it
passes through, and the best score any pathway gets is the maximum match.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 444">
  "Both i and j must increase in value; either i or j must increase by only one
  but the other index may increase by one or more."
</PaperNote>

#### Filling it backward

The trick is to start at the far corner and work back. Each cell asks one
question: of all the cells a pathway could step to next, which holds the
largest number? Then it adds that number to its own. The cells it can step to
are the rest of the row just below it and the rest of the column just to its
right. Their Fig. 1 outlines them. Because you work up from the bottom, those
cells are always done already.

So when the array is full, every number means something.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 445">
  "The number contained in each cell of the array is the largest number of
  identical pairs that can be found if that cell is the origin for a pathway
  which proceeds with increases in running indices."
</PaperNote>

Try it on their own example. The letters are just symbols here, not the
one-letter code. Work up from the bottom row, right to left along each row, as
their Fig. 1 does.

<ArrayFill />

Each cell takes one look along a short row and a short column, and nothing
ever looks at a whole alignment. Jump to Fig. 1 and you're at the cell their
figure shows being worked: R against R, which finds a 4 in the row below and
becomes 5.

The finished array has an 8 in the top left corner. Every pathway has to start
somewhere in the first row or the first column, so the largest number there is
the maximum match.

#### Reading the pathway back

The 8 tells you how good the best alignment is. It doesn't tell you what the
alignment is. For that you follow the numbers back out. Each cell holds its own
1 or 0 plus the number it took from the next cell, so you always know what to
look for: the 8 came from a 7, that 7 from a 6 or a 7, and so on until you run
off the edge.

<ArrayTrace />

At C against C the trail splits. The 6 you need is in two places, J against J
and N against N, and either one leads to 8. One alignment pairs the two J's and
leaves A's N unpaired; the other pairs the N's and leaves B's J unpaired. Their
Fig. 2 draws both pathways, and the paper never says how to choose, because it
doesn't have to. They score the same.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 446">
  "The cells of the array which contributed to the maximum match, may be
  determined by recording the origin of the number that was added to each cell
  when the array was operated upon."
</PaperNote>

#### A real pair

Now the globin fragment from station 1, residues 11–30 of β-hemoglobin against
10–31 of myoglobin. With free gaps the best you could do by hand was 11. The
array gets there in one pass.

<ArrayRun
  across={PUZZLES.globinFree.bottom}
  down={PUZZLES.globinFree.top}
  acrossLabel={PUZZLES.globinFree.bottomLabel}
  downLabel={PUZZLES.globinFree.topLabel}
/>

It finds 11, and 30 different pathways tie for it. That's station 1's free-gap
problem again. When gaps cost nothing, there are lots of equally good ways to
scatter them.

The whole proteins work the same way, just bigger. β-hemoglobin against
myoglobin is an array of 146 × 153 = 22,338 cells, and filling it the 1970 way
reads about 3.3 million cells, since each one scans its row and column. They
ran it on a CDC 3400 (p. 447), and computer time was tight enough that they
held their shuffle test to ten shuffles (p. 448). Still, 3.3 million is nothing
next to 10<sup>88</sup> alignments. The answer is 63.

<LabNote title="Against the paper">
  Filled this way, their example matches Fig. 2 in all 156 cells, with the same
  maximum match of 8 and the same two pathways. β-hemoglobin against myoglobin
  on today's sequences gives 63, as in their Table 1, set 1.
</LabNote>

#### What station 4 leaves open

The array finds the most matches, and station 1 already showed that the most
matches isn't what you want when gaps are free. Needleman and Wunsch's 63
needed at least 35 gaps, and shuffled β-hemoglobin still averaged 55.6. Station
5 puts the penalty inside the array.

### Station 5: The penalty

Station 4's array will take any number of gaps to collect one more match.
That's how β-hemoglobin and myoglobin reached 63, with at least 35 gaps.
Needleman and Wunsch's answer is to make a gap cost something.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 444">
  "A penalty factor, a number subtracted for every gap made, may be assessed as
  a barrier to allowing the gap."
</PaperNote>

Only one rule changes. A step straight along the diagonal pairs the next two
residues and makes no gap, so it stays free. Any other step makes a gap, so it
costs the penalty, however many residues the gap skips. So when you fill a cell,
take the penalty off every cell in the shaded row and column except the one
diagonally next to you. Then pick the largest number, as before.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 447">
  "In proceeding from one cell to the next in a maximum-match pathway, it is
  necessary that the difference between each cell value and the penalty, be
  greater than the value for a cell in a pathway that contains no gap."
</PaperNote>

#### Filling it with a penalty

Here's their example again with a penalty of 1. They never work this example
with a penalty, so these numbers come from their rule, not from a figure in the
paper. Every cell below the one you're filling was filled under the penalty too,
so the numbers are smaller than they were in station 4.

<ArrayFill penalty={1} />

Jump to R against R to see one cell worked. Its own match is worth 1. The
diagonal step is worth 1. The best other step is a 3, which is worth 2 after the
penalty. So the cell is 1 + 2 = 3. In station 4 it was 5.

#### Raising the penalty

Now change the penalty and watch what the whole array picks.

<ArrayPenalty />

With no penalty, the pathway takes four gaps to collect 8 matches. At 1 it gives
up a match or two to drop to one or two gaps. At 3 no gap pays for itself, and
all that's left is 4 matches on a single diagonal. A higher penalty can't change
anything after that, because there are no gaps left to charge for.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 447">
  "If the value of the penalty were zero, all possible gaps could be allowed. If
  the value were equal to the theoretical value for the maximum match between
  two proteins, it would be impossible to allow a gap and the maximum match
  would be the largest of the values found by simply summing along the diagonals
  of the array; this is the simple frame-shift method."
</PaperNote>

#### The real pair

Now β-hemoglobin against myoglobin.

<PenaltySweep />

A penalty of 1 takes the score from 63 to 37 and the gaps from at least 35 to 3.
Two of those three belong together. The pathway leaves its diagonal by skipping
seven residues of myoglobin, then comes back to the same diagonal by skipping
seven residues of β-hemoglobin. That detour costs two gaps and earns four extra
matches. At a penalty of 1 it's worth it, at 2 it ties, and at 3 it's gone.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 450">
  "This penalty will exclude from consideration any possible pathway that leaves
  and returns to a principal diagonal, thereby needing two gaps, in order to add
  only one or two amino acids to the maximum match."
</PaperNote>

After that, one gap is left, and the score is 36 minus the penalty. At 6 that gap
only ties, and from 7 on the score stays at 30, the best plain slide from station
1. The ties shrink along the way, from about 4.4 × 10<sup>18</sup> pathways with
no penalty to 384,256 at a penalty of 1, and to a single pathway at 7.

So why lose all those matches? Because the penalty costs chance more than it
costs real relatives. In their Table 1 the real pair drops from 63 to 38, a loss
of 25. Shuffled β-hemoglobin drops from 55.60 to 27.80, a loss of 27.80. The real
pair's lead over chance grows from 7.40 to 10.20, and their measure of
significance, X, rises from 4.11 to 4.88. Station 7 is about what X means. The
penalty also settles the hand alignment: Braunitzer's two gaps and 37 matches
score 37 − 2 = 35, below the array's 38 (p. 450).

<LabNote title="Against the paper">
  With no penalty, today's sequences match Table 1 exactly: 63 matches and at
  least 35 gaps. With a penalty of 1 they give 37 with 3 gaps, where the paper has
  38 with 4. They used the 1963 β-hemoglobin and 1965 myoglobin sequences, and
  today's differ. That's the likeliest reason for the gap, but those sequences
  aren't on hand, so it's unconfirmed. The paper prints "30 + 4 = 42" on p. 450, a
  misprint for 38 + 4.
</LabNote>

#### What station 5 leaves open

The penalty makes gaps earn their place, but the cells are still crude. A pair
scores 1 if the amino acids are identical and 0 if they aren't, so a near miss
counts the same as a complete mismatch. Needleman and Wunsch's other variable
sets score each pair by how many bases its codons could share. Station 6 runs
them.
