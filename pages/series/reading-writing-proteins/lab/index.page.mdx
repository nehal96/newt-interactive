### Station 1: The problem by hand

By the early 1960s biochemists could read a protein as a string of amino
acids. Myoglobin and the hemoglobin chains had been sequenced, and Kendrew and
Perutz had shown that they fold into almost the same shape. The question was
whether the sequences, read as text, showed the same kinship. Answering it
means lining one sequence up against the other and counting where they agree.
This station is that job done by hand.

The rule for now is the simplest one Needleman and Wunsch used: a column scores
1 when both letters are the same amino acid and 0 otherwise. Each letter is one
amino acid in the one-letter code, which Margaret Dayhoff's group adopted for
their Atlas of protein sequences.

<PaperNote cite="Strasser (2010), p. 639">
  "they adopted a one-letter notation for amino acids, instead of the usual
  three letter code, in order to save computer memory and to make alignments
  more readable on fixed-space printers."
</PaperNote>

#### Sliding

The first thing to try is sliding one sequence along the other. Each position
of the bottom row gives every letter a new partner on top, and the score counts
the columns that agree.

#### One slide isn't enough

Proteins gain and lose residues as they evolve. When one sequence has extra
residues in the middle, the part before them lines up at one slide and the part
after them at another. In this pair the start agrees as it is, and the end
agrees two places over.

<StripPair
  {...PUZZLES.twoSlides}
  solved="The gap marks two residues, K and T, that the top sequence has and the bottom one lacks. Everything before the gap keeps one pairing and everything after it takes another."
/>

A gap records an insertion or a deletion. Dragging a letter right shows what a
gap does: that letter and everything after it move, and everything before it
stays where it was. A slide is the same move made on the first letter, so a
slide is a gap at the start of a row.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 443">
  "Allowance for gaps (Braunitzer, 1965) greatly multiplies the number of
  comparisons that can be made but introduces unnecessary and partial
  comparisons."
</PaperNote>

#### Gaps for free

So far gaps cost nothing. These two sequences were generated at random and are
unrelated, and the best slide scores 3. Try to reach 6 using gaps.

<StripPair
  {...PUZZLES.freeCheat}
  solved="Free gaps doubled the score of two unrelated sequences. A high count proves nothing until you know what chance produces."
/>

The same happens with real proteins. With free gaps, Needleman and Wunsch
matched 63 residues of β-hemoglobin and myoglobin, but they needed at least 35
gaps to do it, and ten shuffled versions of β-hemoglobin averaged 55.6 matches
with about 36 gaps each (Table 1, p. 449). Gibbs and McIntyre, writing about
claims that a protein repeats part of its own sequence, put the danger plainly.

<PaperNote cite="Gibbs and McIntyre (1970), p. 5">
  "few of the claimed repetitions have been tested statistitically [sic], and
  many rely on the addition of gaps to parts of the sequence, and this has
  inherent dangers."
</PaperNote>

#### Charging for gaps

The fix is to charge for each gap, so that a gap has to earn more matches than
it costs. Needleman and Wunsch subtract a fixed amount for every gap, however
long it is. Gaps at the very ends of a row cost nothing, because a path through
their array may start anywhere along its first row or column.

<PaperNote
  cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 444"
  differs="The paper never says end gaps are free. It follows from where their pathways may start and stop (pp. 444, 446)."
>
  "A penalty factor, a number subtracted for every gap made, may be assessed as
  a barrier to allowing the gap… No gap would be allowed in the operation unless
  the benefit from allowing that gap would exceed the barrier."
</PaperNote>

On the whole proteins, a penalty of 1 per gap gave Needleman and Wunsch 38:
42 matches less 4 gaps. Braunitzer's earlier alignment of the same pair used
two gaps and matched 37 residues, which scores 35 under that rule (p. 450).

#### Too many to check

On fragments this short you can find the best alignment by trying things. The
whole proteins are another matter. Here is how many alignments two sequences
have:

<AlignmentCount />

The two counts differ in one detail. When a gap in the top row sits next to a
gap in the bottom row, you can write them in either order; one count treats
those as the same alignment and the other doesn't. Sean Eddy's primer on
dynamic programming uses the first. Either way, β-hemoglobin against myoglobin
has somewhere between 10<sup>88</sup> and 10<sup>113</sup> alignments, far
more than anyone could check by hand or any computer of the 1960s could
enumerate.

<PaperNote cite="Eddy (2004), p. 909">
  "Why do we need a fancy algorithm? Can't we just score all possible
  alignments and pick the best one? This isn't practical".
</PaperNote>

Before 1970, both steps came down to judgement. People compared sequences by
eye to decide where the gaps went, and then judged whether the result meant
anything.

<PaperNote cite="Needleman and Wunsch (1970), p. 443">
  "Because the method of visual comparison is tedious and because the
  determination of the significance of a given result usually is left to
  intuitive rationalization, computer-based statistical approaches have been
  proposed (Fitch, 1966; Needleman & Blair, 1969)."
</PaperNote>

#### What station 1 leaves open

Two questions are left. How do you find the best alignment without trying them
all? That is Needleman and Wunsch's array, in stations 4 and 5. And how do you
tell whether a score is more than chance? That is the shuffle test, in station
7. Four years before Needleman and Wunsch, Walter Fitch avoided the first
question by never placing gaps at all. That is station 2.
