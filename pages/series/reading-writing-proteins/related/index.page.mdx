_Working skeleton. Each heading is one step of the piece; the plan for each is in
`docs/reading-writing-proteins/plan.md`._

### Same shape, different words

_Myoglobin and β-hemoglobin side by side: one fold, about a quarter of their
residues identical. The question the piece asks._

### Sliding one sequence along the other

_The reader slides a strip and keeps score. Each offset they try leaves a bar._

### One slide isn't enough

_A related pair whose start and end want different slides. Pulling a residue opens a gap._

### Gaps for free

_The same move on two unrelated sequences inflates their score. The rule: every gap costs._

### Real globins

_A globin fragment where one gap pays for itself and the rest don't._

### Too many ways to line them up

_The count of possible alignments as the sequences grow, and Braunitzer's alignment by eye._

### Every alignment is a path

_The two strips fold onto the edges of a grid. An alignment becomes a path through it._

### Dots in the grid

_Gibbs and McIntyre's diagram. Raw dots for β against myoglobin, and which of nine plots is real._

### A cell remembers the best it can do

_Needleman and Wunsch's 1970 rule, filled backward from the C-terminus, first by hand and then by the step player._

### Reading off the answer

_The maximum in the first row or column, and the trace back to the alignment._

### Charging for gaps

_The same grid with a penalty. The path straightens._

### The whole proteins

_63 matches with free gaps, 38 with a penalty of one, against Braunitzer's 37._

### Is that more than chance?

_Shuffle β, realign, and build the histogram: ten shuffles as in 1970, then a thousand._

### No best set of numbers

_The scores depend on weights nobody could justify yet, which is the next piece's question._
