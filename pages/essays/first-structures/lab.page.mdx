How do you get from spots on a film to a picture of the molecule that made them?

The way in is to understand how a molecule makes spots in the first place, and then try to run that backwards. Each section below answers one question and ends with the question it raises. Act I is how a molecule makes spots. Act II asks whether the process can be run backwards, and finds what is missing. Act III is how Perutz and Kendrew found it.

<ChainMap />

### Act I: How does a molecule make spots?

<Q n={1} />

A narrow beam of x-rays is aimed at a crystal less than a millimetre across, sealed in a glass tube with a little of the liquid it grew in. Most of the beam passes straight through the crystal and is caught by a small block, the beam stop, so that it doesn’t black out the film. A tiny fraction is knocked off course by the crystal and flies off at every angle. A flat photographic film, 5 to 10 centimetres behind the crystal, catches it.

Each point on the film catches only the x-rays that left the crystal at one particular angle: points near the centre, small angles; points near the edge, large ones. Nobody picks an angle. The film records every point at once, over an exposure that took Perutz’s camera about twelve hours. When it is developed, it shows dark spots in some places and nothing in between.

<Figure title="X-rays that pass through a crystal leave spots on a film behind it">
  <Camera />
</Figure>

<Next n={2}>Something travels from the crystal to the film and lands only in some places. What is it?</Next>

<Q n={2} />

An x-ray is a wave, like light but with a much shorter wavelength: about one ångström (Å), a ten-billionth of a metre, which is roughly the distance between neighbouring atoms in a molecule. The copper tubes of Perutz’s day gave x-rays 1.54 Å long.

Besides its wavelength, a wave has two properties that matter here. Its **height** sets how much it exposes the film: the darkness goes with the height squared, so a wave twice as tall makes a mark four times as dark. Its **phase** says where its crests fall, measured from some fixed starting line. Sliding a wave along changes its phase and nothing else; on its own, the film can’t tell the difference.

<Figure
  title="A wave’s height sets how dark it makes the film; its phase only moves its crests"
  subtitle="Raise the wave and the film darkens with the height squared. Slide it and the film doesn’t change."
>
  <OneWave />
</Figure>

<Next n={3}>One wave on its own exposes the film evenly. Spots need something more. What happens when two waves arrive at the same place?</Next>

<Q n={3} />

They add. At every point, the combined wave is the sum of the two waves’ heights there, the way two sets of ripples on a pond add where they cross.

If the two waves’ crests line up, they are **in step**, and the sum is twice as tall: four times as dark on the film. If the crests of one fall on the troughs of the other, they cancel, and nothing is left. Anything in between gives something in between. So whether two equal waves leave a dark mark or no mark at all depends only on how their crests line up, which is to say, on their phases.

  subtitle="Drag wave 2, or use the slider."
>

</Figure>

<Next n={4}>So the film turns dark where waves arrive in step and stays clear where they cancel. But where do the waves arriving at the film come from?</Next>

<Q n={4} />

From the atoms in the crystal. As an x-ray wave passes over an atom, it shakes the atom’s electrons, and each shaken electron sends out a new, weak wave of the same wavelength in every direction, like ripples spreading from a stone dropped in water. The more electrons an atom has, the stronger its wave: mercury has 80, carbon 6.

A single atom spreads its ripples evenly in every direction, so it leaves a faint, even fog on the film, with no spots.

<Figure title="One atom sends out ripples in every direction and fogs the film evenly">
  <OneAtom />
</Figure>

<Next n={5}>One atom gives no spots. What happens with two, when their ripples meet at the film?</Next>

<Q n={5} />

Each point on the film now receives two waves, one from each atom. The beam reaches both atoms at the same moment, so their waves set off in step. But to reach a given point on the film they travel different distances, so they need not arrive in step.

At the centre of the film both paths are the same length, so the two waves arrive in step and add. Move along the film and the path from one atom grows longer than the path from the other. Where the extra path is a whole number of wavelengths, the waves are back in step and the film is dark. Where it is half a wavelength, or one and a half, they cancel and the film stays clear. So the film is striped with bands.

Move the atoms farther apart and the extra path grows faster as you move along the film, so the bands crowd closer together.

  caption="Not to scale. Waves from the two atoms are drawn equally tall."
>

</Figure>

<Next n={6}>Two atoms give broad, soft bands. A crystal’s rows run to tens of thousands of copies. What do many atoms in a row do?</Next>

<Q n={6} />

Add more atoms at the same spacing. At the dark bands, each atom’s wave arrives a whole number of wavelengths behind its neighbour’s, so all of them arrive in step and the bands stay exactly where they were. Just beside a band, each wave lags only slightly behind the one before, but the lags add up along the row until the waves are spread right around the cycle and cancel. So the bands get narrower.

They also get much darker. With every wave in step, the heights add: a row of 12 atoms gives a wave 12 times as tall, and 144 times as dark, as one atom alone.

  subtitle="Add atoms at the same spacing."

<Next n={7}>A real crystal isn’t one row. It repeats in every direction. What does that do to the bands?</Next>

<Q n={7} />

A crystal is one small box of atoms, the **unit cell**, repeated over and over in every direction, like bricks. A row running up and down makes bands that run across the film; a row running across makes bands that run up and down. A crystal has both at once, so the film can be dark only where the two sets of bands cross: at spots.

<Figure
  title="A grid of copies turns bands into spots"
  caption="Not to scale. Each crystal is a single flat layer, seen along the beam."
>
  <CrystalRecords part="spots" />
</Figure>

This is why proteins have to be crystallised. A single molecule scatters far too little to record, and many molecules lying about at random only add up to a blur. Only when a great many copies are lined up, all facing the same way, do their waves reinforce into spots strong enough to photograph.

<Figure title="The same molecules at random give a blur; in a crystal, sharp spots">
  <OrderMakesSpots />
</Figure>

Where the spots fall depends only on how far apart the boxes are. Bigger boxes give more closely spaced bands, and so more closely spaced spots; smaller boxes spread them out. Run the other way, the spacing of the spots on the film gives the size of the box. That is how Perutz measured hemoglobin’s unit cell from his first photographs in 1937–38: about 109 by 63 by 54 Å.

  <ReadingTheFilm group="box" />
</Figure>

<Payoff>The positions of the spots measure the unit cell.</Payoff>

<Next n={8}>But the positions say nothing about what is inside the box. Put a different molecule in the same box and the spots land in exactly the same places. So where does the molecule show?</Next>

<Q n={8} />

In how dark the spots are. Every box in the crystal is identical, so at a spot the boxes’ waves all arrive in step and simply stack up. But what each box sends to that spot depends on its atoms. Each atom in the box sends its own wave there, and each travels its own distance to get there, so the waves arrive shifted by different amounts. What reaches the film is their sum.

The atoms are the same at every spot, so why do their waves arrive shifted by different amounts? It is the two atoms of section 5 again. The beam reaches all the atoms at once, but to reach a spot, the wave from a lower atom has farther to go than the wave from a higher one. That extra path depends on the angle: the farther out the spot, the steeper the angle and the longer the extra path. So the same pair of atoms might be a quarter of a wave apart at one spot, half a wave at the next, three-quarters at the next.

How much the extra path matters depends only on what is left over after whole waves. A wave exactly one, or two, wavelengths behind is back in step (section 3), so an atom 1.74 waves farther arrives just as one 0.74 waves farther would.

Where the extra paths happen to leave the waves nearly in step, the spot is dark. Where they leave them spread around the cycle, they cancel and the spot is faint. Move an atom and its extra path changes at every spot, by a different amount at each, so every darkness changes in its own way. Each spot asks a slightly different question about where the atoms sit, which is why, together, the darknesses can pin down the molecule.

A real protein’s box holds thousands of atoms, not three, so every spot is the sum of thousands of waves. Two different molecules in boxes of the same size give spots in the same places, with different darknesses.

<Figure title="Different molecules in the same box: same spots, different darknesses">
  <CrystalRecords part="darkness" />
</Figure>

<Next n={9}>The darknesses rise and fall from spot to spot. Is there a pattern to them?</Next>

<Q n={9} />

Take one box’s atoms on their own, without the rest of the crystal. They would send some brightness to every point on the film, not just to the spots, and that brightness would rise and fall smoothly from point to point as the atoms’ waves drift into and out of step. That smooth pattern belongs to the molecule (chart 1 below).

The crystal adds a second factor: how well the boxes line up at each point (chart 2). With many boxes, that is zero almost everywhere and large only at the spot positions. The film records the two multiplied together (chart 3). So each spot is a sample of the molecule’s own smooth pattern, taken at a point the crystal allows: a spot that falls where the pattern is low comes out faint, and a bright stretch between spots never shows at all.

With a single box, the film would show the molecule’s whole pattern, far too faint to photograph. Add boxes and it breaks up into spots.

<Payoff>The darknesses of the spots are a coded picture of the molecule: samples of the pattern its atoms make.</Payoff>

<Next n={10}>So the spots hold the molecule, in code. Can we decode it, running all of this backwards from the darknesses to the atoms?</Next>

### Act II: Can we run it backwards?

<Q n={10} />

Act I ran forwards: atoms sit in the cell; each atom’s wave travels its own extra path to each spot; the waves add; the height of the sum sets how dark the spot is (section 8). Running it backwards means starting from the darknesses and finding where the atoms must be.

The plainest way is to guess. Put atoms somewhere, work out each one’s extra path to each spot, add up their waves, and see whether every sum comes out as tall as the film says it should be. If a spot comes out wrong, the guess is wrong. Here two of the three atoms are given; slide the third until every spot matches.

<Figure
  title="Guess, add up the waves, compare with the film"
  subtitle="Slide the middle atom. Pick a spot to see its three waves add up; the dashed lines mark the height the film needs. Red rings mark spots that don’t match."
  caption="The same three-atom cell as Act I. Two of the atoms are taken as known, so there is one position to guess."
>
  <GuessAtom />
</Figure>

Slide the atom all the way across, though, and two positions make all six spots match: 35% across the cell, where the atom really is, and 47%.

With one atom to place along one line, guessing takes seconds, and for molecules of a few dozen atoms it is how crystallographers worked. But hemoglobin has about 5,000 atoms, each placed in three dimensions. The arrangements to try outnumber anything that could ever be checked, and a near miss gives no clue which way to move: slide the atom above and the difference rises and falls all across the cell, dropping to zero only at the two right places.

<Next n={11}>Guessing can’t work for a protein. Is there a way back that doesn’t guess?</Next>

<Q n={11} />

There is, and it starts by turning each spot back into something you can lay over the box.

Take spot 3 and slide a single atom slowly across the box. As it moves, its wave has a little farther to travel to the spot (section 8), so it slides out of step with a wave from the box’s left wall, then back into step, then out again. Whenever the extra distance reaches a whole wavelength, it is back in step. At spot 3 that happens three times on the way across.

Mark every place where the atom’s wave arrives in step. The marks are evenly spaced, because the extra distance grows steadily as the atom moves, and in a flat box each mark is a line, so together they are stripes. They are a map of where atoms would light up spot 3. A dark spot means many of the molecule’s atoms sit on its stripes; a faint one, that they sit between them.

<Figure
  title="Wherever an atom’s wave reaches the spot in step, mark a stripe"
  subtitle="Slide the atom across the box and watch its wave at spot 3."
  caption="The same box as section 10, drawn flat so that its marks show as lines."
>
  <SpotStripes />
</Figure>

Every spot has its own stripes. The farther out the spot, the faster the extra distance grows as the atom moves, so the finer its stripes.

<Next n={12}>So each spot gives a sheet of stripes, as bold as the spot is dark. What happens when you stack the sheets?</Next>

<Q n={12} />

Draw each spot’s stripes on a clear sheet, as bold as the spot is dark, and stack the sheets over the box. Where many bold stripes land on top of one another the stack is darkest, and those places are the atoms: every atom sits on the stripes of every spot it helped to light. One sheet gives a blur. Six put three dark bands on the three atoms.

But each sheet has to be slid to the right place first. A spot’s place on the film says how fine its stripes are, and its darkness how bold. Neither says where the stripes sit across the box. That is the phase: where the spot’s wave has its crests (section 2). Slide the very same sheets to the wrong places and the dark bands land in the wrong places, a picture of a molecule that isn’t there.

<Figure
  title="The same sheets, stacked in the right places and in the wrong ones"
  subtitle="Stack the sheets one at a time. Both sides use the same sheets; only where each is slid differs."
  caption="Darkest where the most bold stripes overlap. The wrong slides here belong to a molecule with atoms at 22%, 52% and 88%; any wrong slides give a wrong picture."
>
  <StackSheets />
</Figure>

Nothing on the film says which stack is right: it records how fine and how bold each sheet is, never where it goes. With a few atoms you can get round that by guessing (section 10). With 5,000, Perutz had every sheet and no way to lay them down. That is what the essay means by “he did not know how to line them up.”

<Payoff>Each spot is a sheet of stripes, and stacked in the right places the sheets draw the molecule. The film records how fine and how bold each sheet is, but not where it goes. That missing slide is the phase, and finding it is the phase problem.</Payoff>

<Next n={13}>So each spot’s wave has to be put the right way round, and the film doesn’t say which. What is that like to do?</Next>

<Q n={13} />

Here is the lining up as a game. The molecule this time is symmetric, the same read from either end, which makes each spot’s wave simpler: it can sit only two ways, with a crest at the middle of the box or a trough there. The film says how tall each wave is. It doesn’t say which way up. (In general a wave could sit anywhere, slid by any amount, as in section 12; a symmetric molecule leaves it only these two choices, a flip rather than a slide.)

Flip the waves and watch the picture they add up to. Each line-up draws a different molecule, and the film fits every one of them.

<Figure
  title="Every way of lining up the waves draws a molecule. Which is the real one?"
  subtitle="Each button flips one spot’s wave. The film never changes. When you have a guess, show the real atoms."
  caption="A symmetric three-atom molecule, so each wave has either a crest or a trough at the middle. The wave heights come from the spots’ darkness."
>
  <LineUp />

Nothing on the film picks out the right picture. With four spots you can try all sixteen, but each extra spot doubles the count, and a protein’s film has thousands. Perutz had no dots to check against, only a near-endless pile of pictures that all fit his film equally well.

<Next n={14}>So the flips have to be found some other way. The first idea was to add a wave you can work out.</Next>

### Act III: How did they find the phases?

<Q n={14} />

Add something to the crystal whose wave you can work out. The simplest is one heavy atom, put where you know it sits. Because you know where it is, you know where its wave’s crests fall at every spot (section 8): that is what makes its wave known.

Now compare the film before and after. Where the protein’s wave is in step with the heavy atom’s, the two add and the spot darkens. Where it is opposed, they partly cancel and the spot fades. One comparison of two films tells you, spot by spot, which way up each wave goes.

Perutz first did this in 1943, with the liquid between the molecules as the known part: soaking in salt changed its wave by an amount he could work out, though only for one column of spots. In 1953 he used mercury. He didn’t place the mercury atoms himself; he found where they had settled from how the spots changed, which one heavy atom is simple enough to allow. From there he got the flips for a whole flat view of the crystal.

<Next n={15}>In 1943 the known part was the liquid, not an atom. Could its wave be worked out?</Next>

<Q n={15} />

Only for some spots. A heavy atom is easy: one atom, one place. The liquid between the molecules fills gaps of shapes nobody knew, so from the side its wave couldn’t be worked out. But seen straight down through the layers, averaged across each depth, the uneven gaps even out, and the liquid is just a flat layer of known thickness and saltiness. One column of spots through the centre of the film records exactly that view, so only for those spots could the liquid’s wave be worked out, and only for those could the salt test give the flips.

For the seven spots of that column nearest the centre, Perutz got the flips (two of them from the drying photographs instead). Then he could do what the game in section 13 asks: add the seven waves, each the right way up. Because these spots look straight down through the layers, the sum isn’t a picture but a single line: how much matter lies at each depth through one layer. It had four bumps about nine ångströms apart, which he read as four sheets of protein chain. That reading would turn out to be wrong.

<Next n={16}>Seven flips gave one line. A picture needed spots in every direction, and their flips were still unknown. Can you get anything without them?</Next>

<Q n={16} />

Something, yes. Add the waves using only their heights, as though every wave had its crests at the same starting point, and the result is not a map of the atoms but a map of the distances between them: a Patterson map. It needs only the darkness of the spots, so Perutz could make one from his photographs alone.

The essay explains it with a dark classroom. You can’t see where the children sit, but you have a pack of notes, each saying how far apart one pair is and in which direction: “Sam is 1 step to the right of Ali.” Mark a dot in the middle of a whiteboard, and for each note, walk its steps from the dot and put a sticker where you land. If the desks are in neat rows, many pairs are the same steps apart, so many notes send you to the same places and the stickers pile up. If the children are scattered, the stickers land all over the board and no pattern stands out.

A Patterson map is built the same way: each pair of atoms adds a peak, and the more pairs share a spacing, the taller the peak grows. That is what made it a test. Perutz’s flat maps of 1947 showed only the molecule’s outline, so he set out to make one in three dimensions, to see whether hemoglobin’s chains lay side by side. If they did, the same spacings would repeat thousands of times and pile up into straight rods; if they were tangled, the map would be a blur.

By the spring of 1948, after measuring about 7,000 spots and some 400 million additions, he had it: 31 slices, traced onto transparent sheets and stacked. The rods were there. He read them as chains lying side by side in four layers, matching his four sheets of 1943. It looked like confirmation. It would turn out to be wrong: a few years later, Crick showed the rods were far too faint for straight chains.

<Next n={17}>Distances weren’t enough. The next idea was the heavy atom of section 14, for real.</Next>

<Q n={17} />

In 1953 a letter from Austen Riggs showed that mercury could be fixed to hemoglobin without changing its shape. Perutz tried it. On the photographs the spots fell in exactly the same places, but many were darker or fainter. Comparing the two films showed where the mercury sat, so its wave in every spot could be worked out: the known wave of section 14. Whether each spot darkened or faded gave its flip.

Bragg and Perutz added the signed waves into the first electron density map of a protein built from measured phases. It came from one flat view, so it was flat, and too blurred to show chains, but it settled one thing: there were no regularly spaced layers. The four sheets were gone.

<Next n={18}>Flips only worked for one flat view. What about the rest of the spots?</Next>

<Q n={18} />

For the rest, each wave could be slid by any amount, not just flipped (section 12). One heavy atom then tells you how far a spot’s crests lie from the heavy atom’s, but not whether ahead or behind: two possible slides. In 1954 Dorothy Hodgkin pointed out the fix. A second heavy atom, in a different place, gives a second known wave, and only one slide fits both.

<Next n={19}>With two or more heavy atoms, every spot’s wave could be put in place. What did the map show?</Next>

<Q n={19} />

Kendrew got there first, with myoglobin, a quarter of hemoglobin’s size, and five heavy-atom versions of its crystals. Every spot’s wave could now be put in its right place, and added up, as in section 13 but in three dimensions, on Cambridge’s EDSAC computer. The more spots added, the sharper the map: the innermost give only the outline, and each ring further out adds finer detail.
