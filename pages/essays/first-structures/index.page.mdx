AI tools can predict the shape of a protein solely from its sequence, with high accuracy. [AlphaFold’s database](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) holds more than 200 million of these predicted structures, and other AI tools even help researchers predict how drug molecules bind to proteins, discern which mutations might cause disease, or design entirely new proteins from scratch.

These tools were trained on the Protein Data Bank (PDB), a large repository of 3D proteins containing more than 250,000 experimentally-determined structures. When it launched in 1971, the PDB held [just seven structures](https://www.rcsb.org/pages/about-us/history), including myoglobin and hemoglobin, which were solved in Cambridge in 1957 and 1959 by John Kendrew and Max Perutz, respectively.

[About 80](https://rcsb.org/statistics/growth/experimental-method) percent of all structures in the PDB were solved using a method called x-ray crystallography; x-ray beams are launched at a crystal of packed proteins, and then researchers deduce the shapes of proteins *within* that crystal by looking at the spots left behind on a photographic film after these x-rays scatter and collide.

When Perutz took his first photographs of hemoglobin crystals in 1937, he had his diffraction data as spots on film, but no way to turn those spots into a three-dimensional picture of the molecule. At the time, no one knew whether the structure of a protein could be measured at all. But Perutz set out to solve a protein’s structure anyway, on what ended up being a 22-year journey that included a world war, exile, years of misleading results, creeping doubt and, finally, a breakthrough prompted by an unexpected letter.

### X-Rays

Max Perutz joined the lab of J.D. Bernal, an x-ray crystallographer in Cambridge, by chance. As a student in Vienna, he had asked his chemistry professor, Hermann Mark, who was traveling to Cambridge, to inquire about a spot in a biochemistry lab, but the professor came back with a place under Bernal instead. When Perutz protested that he knew nothing of crystallography, the professor told him, “You’ll learn it, my boy.”

Bernal’s research group had only recently begun using x-ray crystallography to solve *biological* molecules, rather than minerals and metals, such as graphite and bronze. In 1934, he and his research student, Dorothy Crowfoot, [produced](https://www.nature.com/articles/133794b0) the first x-ray diffraction photograph of the protein crystal pepsin, overturning the widely held belief that proteins would not diffract x-ray beams. Crowfoot recalled in her [1980 memoir of Bernal](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbm.1980.0002) that on the night of the first photographs, Bernal wandered through the streets of Cambridge, “thinking of the future and of how much it might be possible to know about the structure of proteins if the photographs… could be interpreted in every detail.”

When Perutz arrived in Cambridge in 1936, he found the lab in a small, dingy sub-department in the Cavendish Laboratory, then part of the physics department. Bernal set him to work on minerals from blast-furnace slag, which taught Perutz the basics of x-ray crystallography.

The technique works by shooting a narrow beam of x-rays at a crystal, which scatters them into a grid of spots on photographic film placed behind it. In principle, from where the spots fall and how dark they are, one can work back to where the atoms sit in the crystal.

X-rays are waves, with a wavelength — the distance between crests — roughly comparable to the distance between atoms in a molecule. Like other waves, they have *amplitude* (height), which sets brightness, and *phase*, which describes where along its cycle a wave is. When waves are added, those in step — like crest to crest — reinforce each other, and those opposed — like crest to trough — cancel each other out.

Spots form because of this wave addition. When x-rays strike a molecule, its electrons send out new waves of their own, in every direction. A single molecule scatters far too little to record, but in a crystal, billions of identical copies are stacked like bricks in every direction. Each brick, the small box holding one or a few molecules, is called the “<Term k="unitCell">unit cell</Term>.”

<Sketch caption="One molecule scatters too little to record. A crystal stacks identical copies of it, one unit cell after another.">
  <UnitCell />
</Sketch>

Because they’re identical, every unit cell sends out the same waves, but each from a different starting point. In most directions, the *extra* distance one cell’s wave travels compared with its neighbor’s is some awkward fraction of a wavelength, so waves from billions of cells arrive at every possible offset and cancel each other out. Only in a few directions is that extra distance a *whole* number of wavelengths. There, each cell’s waves arrive exactly in step with its neighbor’s, and so with all the others, and together they add up to be strong enough to darken the film into a spot.

<Sketch caption="Move the point along the film. Only where each cell’s wave travels a whole number of wavelengths farther than its neighbor’s do the waves add up to a spot. With billions of cells, nothing is left in between.">
  <Directions />
</Sketch>

How dark each spot is depends on what is *inside* the unit cell. In the direction of each spot, every atom in the cell sends out its own small wave. The more electrons the atom has, the stronger the wave. Depending on where the atoms sit, their waves travel slightly different distances and arrive with different offsets, where they combine into a single wave for that spot. When most of them reinforce each other, the combined wave is tall and the spot is dark; when they mostly cancel, the spot is faint. Each direction has a different set of offsets, so each spot has its own darkness. Thus, the positions of the spots measure the unit cell, and the darkness of the spots is a coded picture of the molecule inside.

</Sketch>

### The Big Idea

By the time Perutz began using this method, x-ray crystallography had been used to solve the structures of minerals, metals and organic molecules of a few dozen atoms, but nothing anywhere as large as a protein.

Regardless, after tiring of blast-furnace slag, Perutz decided to try x-ray crystallography for proteins. In the summer of 1937, while visiting Prague, he raised the idea with his cousin’s husband, a young professor named Felix Haurowitz. Perutz was considering hemin, a small salt found in hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in the blood. But Haurowitz talked him out of it and told him to go for the *whole* hemoglobin protein instead, also suggesting that a physiologist in Cambridge, Gilbert Adair, could provide hemoglobin crystals.

Perutz approached Adair back in Cambridge, and the physiologist grew him crystals of horse methemoglobin—a stable, oxidized form of hemoglobin—apologizing that they were “probably much too small.” Yet they turned out to be excellent for x-ray work<Fn>Perutz had also been given crystals of chymotrypsin, a digestive enzyme sent from the Rockefeller Institute, but they were twinned — two lattices grown through one another — so there was no telling which spots belonged to which. Adair’s hemoglobin gave clean single crystals, and so it became, by elimination, Perutz’s sole focus.</Fn>.

By the winter of 1937–38, Perutz got his first encouraging results. The photographs of Adair’s wet crystals gave sharp spots right up to the edge of the film, showing that every hemoglobin molecule in the crystal was identical to every other, right down to the positions of their atoms. From the spacing of the spots near the center of the film, Perutz measured the unit cell; and from the symmetry of the pattern, he showed that each molecule was made of identical halves.

Perutz published these results in [a letter to *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/141523a0) on 19 March 1938<Fn>Written with Bernal and Isidor Fankuchen, an American crystallographer. From the unit cell’s volume and the crystal’s density, Perutz worked out that each cell held two molecules, each weighing about 69,000 times as much as a hydrogen atom, close to the figures Adair and Svedberg had measured by other methods.</Fn>. The problem, however, was that the film recorded only part of the information needed to fully reconstruct the molecule. Each spot’s darkness told Perutz how strong a scattered x-ray wave was, but it did not reveal the wave’s phase. The phases are critical because two waves of equal strength can produce completely different results when added together.

To work backward from the spots to a picture of the molecule, Perutz needed to add the waves back together, a calculation known as a Fourier synthesis. This required both the height and the phase of each wave; without the phase, he did not know how to line them up, and different choices of phase could produce different pictures from the same spots. For smaller molecules, crystallographers could work around this by guessing an arrangement of atoms, calculating the spots it would produce, and comparing them with the film, but for a molecule with thousands of atoms, there were far too many arrangements to try. Finding the missing phases was the “phase problem,” and it held up solving hemoglobin for the next 15 years.

But there were other, more pressing matters holding Perutz back. On 12 March—the week before the *Nature* paper was published—Hitler annexed Austria. German troops marched into Vienna, and Perutz’s parents, Jewish by descent, fled to Prague. Stranded in Cambridge, Perutz could no longer safely return home, and his parents could no longer fund his studies<Fn>Amid this turmoil, Perutz still found a way to spend the summer in the mountains. A skier and mountaineer since childhood, he joined an expedition to the Swiss Alps to study how snow turns into glacier ice. The work, published the following year, gave him a reputation as a glacier expert. In 1943 that reputation took him onto Habakkuk, a wartime project to build an aircraft carrier out of pykrete, ice mixed with wood pulp, an idea of the journalist and inventor Geoffrey Pyke. Perutz tested it in a refrigerated meat store beneath London’s Smithfield Market, hidden behind frozen carcasses, and found that it slowly sagged unless kept below about −16 °C. Keeping a ship that cold would have taken so much steel that, as Perutz put it, the U.S. Navy decided “Habakkuk was a false prophet.”</Fn>.

That autumn, in 1938, Lawrence Bragg, the leading figure in x-ray crystallography, was appointed head of the Cavendish, and Perutz’s supervisor, Bernal, moved to Birkbeck College in London, “taking all of biological crystallography with him except Perutz”<Fn>Bernal had been blocked from the Cambridge chair partly by his membership of the Communist Party.</Fn>. Perutz was nervous about whether his new boss would support the hemoglobin project, but when he mustered the courage to ask weeks later, Bragg said Perutz had taken “the best photographs of a protein at that time.” The praise “fired” Perutz’s enthusiasm. Bragg also secured a grant from the Rockefeller Foundation to support Perutz’s research<Fn>Bragg’s words come from an interview with Horace Freeland Judson for *The Eighth Day of Creation*. The grant gave Perutz a paid position, which let him support his parents and bring them to England; they had fled from Prague to Switzerland after Czechoslovakia came under German pressure.</Fn>.

In January 1939, Bernal gave [a lecture](https://doi.org/10.1038/143663a0) on the phase problem in London. Direct analysis of x-ray photographs, he said, “is rendered impossible by the fact that we can never know the phases of the reflections corresponding to the different spots.” He speculated on two ways to solve this. First, he said, perhaps it was possible to recover some of the missing phase information by watching how the diffraction pattern changed as the crystal dried. Protein crystals contain water between their molecules, and as it leaves, the crystal can shrink, bringing the molecules closer together and changing how their scattered waves reinforce or cancel one another. If the molecules themselves kept their shape, comparing photographs at different stages of drying might help narrow down which phases could account for the changing intensities.

The second idea, which Bernal only briefly mentioned, was to add a heavy atom to the molecule<Fn>The idea was not new. Three years earlier, J. Monteath Robertson had worked out the complete structure of phthalocyanine, a blue dye of fifty-eight atoms, by comparing two crystals of it, one with a nickel atom at the center of the molecule and one without. The nickel changed how dark the spots were, and from those changes Robertson could work out where every atom sat.</Fn>, which would scatter x-rays strongly because of its many electrons. If its position in the crystal was known, then the waves it contributed could be calculated, including their phases, and used as a reference to read the protein’s wave.

Perutz decided to try the first path. But in September 1939, Britain declared war on Germany, and in May 1940, just a few weeks after he received his PhD, Perutz, an Austrian whom a tribunal had cleared as a refugee from Nazi oppression, was arrested when the Home Office extended internment to men previously judged harmless. Britain sent him to Canada as part of its wartime policy of overseas internment, and Perutz was held in a camp of wooden huts above Quebec City, where the internees slept, a hundred men to a room, double-bunked, behind barbed-wire windows. His colleagues campaigned for his release, and in January 1941 he landed at Liverpool and was told to register with the Cambridge police as an [enemy alien](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1985/08/12/enemy-alien)<Fn>When war broke out, tribunals had sorted German and Austrian nationals into security risks, doubtful cases and refugees, and those in the last group had been left free to carry on their work. Perutz told the whole story of his internment in “Enemy Alien,” *The New Yorker*, 12 August 1985.</Fn>.

### The Phase Problem

Back in Cambridge, Perutz returned to Bernal’s idea of drying crystals. After taking photographs at four stages of a slow-drying process, Perutz found that the crystal contracted almost entirely in one direction and, from this, concluded that the protein molecules must pack into layers that moved closer together as the water between them evaporated. He [published these results](https://doi.org/10.1038/149491a0) in *Nature* in May 1942. Using these four photographs, he incorrectly concluded that each molecule was flat, “a platelet 36 Å thick.”

The drying photographs, though, yielded very few phases. By 1943, after combining drying measurements with experiments that changed the crystals’ salt content, Perutz and Joy Boyes-Watson deduced the phases of just seven spots, all lying along one line on the film. That was enough to draw a graph of how electrons were distributed through the thickness of a protein layer. The graph, [published in 1943](https://doi.org/10.1038/151714a0), had four peaks, which Perutz interpreted as four thin sheets of protein chain stacked on one another. But it was one-dimensional; it could not show how the chains connected in three dimensions.

To get a three-dimensional map of electron density, Perutz needed to find the phases for the thousands of other spots, but there was no way to do that yet. So, Perutz turned to calculating a different kind of map, one that didn’t require phases. Using just intensities, a Patterson map showed distances and directions between pairs of atoms, rather than where the atoms themselves sat. For a molecule like hemoglobin, with thousands of atoms, this kind of map would be crowded, but Perutz hoped that if the chains inside did in fact run in straight, parallel lines, then the distances between them would repeat often enough and stand out as straight rods. Measuring the 7,000 or so spots this map required was a task “whose length and tediousness it will be better not to describe,” he wrote<Fn>With no instrument to read darkness, Perutz’s assistants Joy Boyes-Watson and Edna Davidson graded each spot by eye against a calibration strip, a row of about twenty test exposures running from barely visible to nearly black. Recording a single layer of spots took eighteen photographs, and their estimates could be off by 20–25% for strong spots and 30–40% for weak ones.</Fn>.

<Sketch caption="A Patterson map needs no phases. Each peak marks one pair of atoms: how far apart they are, and in which direction. It does not show where the atoms themselves sit.">

</Sketch>

The finished Patterson map, in 1948, showed what Perutz hoped for: the rods were there, as long, straight ridges, which he read as evidence of chains lying side by side in four layers<Fn>Adding up the waves was beyond any machine in Cambridge, so the sums went to the Scientific Computing Service in London, which ran them on punched-card machines using a procedure Dorothy Crowfoot’s group in Oxford had devised for penicillin. In all, the calculation took some 400 million additions, and the answer came back as 31 contour maps, one for each slice through the unit cell.</Fn>. But the ridges simply meant that the distances between atoms recurred unusually often, and parallel chains were just one of the many arrangements that could have produced them.

On 8 June 1948, Perutz [submitted his findings](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1949.0005) to the Royal Society. The “parallel rods” structure, he wrote, was “plausible and consistent with all the known evidence,” but proof for the time being eluded him, and he ended the paper by wondering whether hemoglobin could be taken any further at all.

---

Partway through those years, early in 1946, Perutz got a new partner. John Kendrew, a physical chemist, had met Bernal while they were stationed in wartime Ceylon, who persuaded him to work on proteins. He joined Cambridge after, where Perutz quickly taught him everything he knew about crystallography.

In June 1948, at a symposium on hemoglobin, the two of them [reviewed](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.6422) the methods crystallographers had used to find phases for smaller molecules. One method relied on attaching a heavy atom to the molecule, one that scattered so strongly that it largely dominated the phases. They dismissed this for proteins: even mercury’s 80 electrons would make a negligible contribution against hemoglobin’s tens of thousands<Fn>Perutz also knew that Dorothy Crowfoot had tried adding heavy atoms to insulin, a much smaller protein, and seen no detectable change.</Fn>. Another approach, isomorphous replacement, compared photographs of crystals with and without a heavy atom, where the heavy atom only needed to change the spots by a detectable amount, rather than completely dominate. But no one had yet attached such an atom to a protein without ruining the crystal.

Then, in the autumn of 1949, Francis Crick joined the Cavendish, where he quickly learned crystallography largely by arguing with Perutz and Kendrew. He decided to check their assumption that hemoglobin was made up of straight and parallel chains. If that were true, he calculated, the ridges in the 1948 map should have been much stronger. Instead, his calculations suggested that the chains could run straight for only about a dozen amino acids before turning a corner.

In 1951, Crick presented these findings in a talk titled *What Mad Pursuit*, essentially telling Perutz and Kendrew that their assumptions for the last near-decade were incorrect. As Perutz would put it many years later: “Here was this new fellow… [who] was telling us that what we were so proud of, was wrong.”

---

Salvation arrived in 1953, in the form of a letter penned by Austen Riggs, a young biochemist at Harvard. Riggs enclosed [a paper](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.36.1.1) showing that he could attach a mercury compound to hemoglobin’s cysteines — amino acids containing sulphur — while preserving its cooperative uptake of oxygen: binding the first oxygen still helped the protein bind the next, meaning its function had not been destroyed<Fn>Riggs wrote after reading Perutz’s two short letters on sickle-cell hemoglobin. In his experiments, only two of hemoglobin’s six cysteines took up the mercury, which he took to mean that two lay on the surface of the molecule, where the mercury could reach them, and four were buried inside.</Fn>.

“I jumped when I saw that,” Perutz said years later, “because it was clear to me that if it left the biological properties intact, then it would also leave the structure intact.” If the protein kept its shape, it would also pack into crystals just as before, with the mercury at the same place in every unit cell. Two crystals identical except for a heavy atom were exactly what isomorphous replacement needed.

There was still the question that had made Perutz and Kendrew dismiss heavy atom methods in 1948: could one atom among thousands make any difference? By 1951, Perutz had used a Geiger counter to measure how strong hemoglobin’s spots actually were, and they turned out to be weaker than expected. At each spot, the waves scattered by the protein’s electrons largely cancelled each other out, leaving a much weaker combined wave. Mercury’s 80 electrons could therefore make a sizable difference: a spot became nearly twice as dark, or half as dark, depending on whether the mercury’s wave lined up with the protein or against it<Fn>A reminder of the difference: in the heavy-atom method, one atom is so heavy that its wave dominates every spot, so each spot’s phase is close to that atom’s own, which can be calculated once the atom is located. No atom is heavy enough to do that in a protein.</Fn>.

Riggs had worked with human hemoglobin, which didn’t form crystals suited to x-ray work, so Perutz’s lab had to get mercury onto horse hemoglobin. Chemical tests showed that it bound to two cysteines, presumably one on each of its identical halves, but they didn’t know *where* those cysteines were located.

Perutz photographed and developed films of plain and mercury-treated crystals in the summer of 1953, and as he later told it, ran to fetch Bragg down to the darkroom to see them side by side. The spots fell as they had before, which meant the molecules packed identically, but many were darker or fainter<Fn>A control crystal grown from the same protein without mercury showed the normal pattern, so the differences could only come from the heavy atoms.</Fn>. It was the first time a heavy atom had measurably changed the diffraction pattern of a protein.

Over the following months, Perutz converted those intensity changes into phases. To do this, first he had to find where the mercury sat. For that, he measured how much the spots had changed between the two films — essentially, the change the mercury made. From these *differences*, he calculated a Patterson map. This time, with only two atoms to show instead of thousands of protein atoms, it gave one clear peak: the distance and direction between the two mercury atoms. After ruling out a few alternatives, Perutz placed them 30Å apart, one on each half of the molecule<Fn>The difference Patterson map left eight possible positions for the mercury pair. Two checks narrowed them to one. The mercury had to sit inside the molecule’s outline, which Perutz had measured in 1952, and it had to change four spots whose phases were already known in the direction the films showed. Those four phases had come from the drying and salt experiments, so the years that had yielded so little else supplied the check. Bragg later called that earlier work scaffolding, “knocked away when the building was completed.”</Fn>.

</Wrap>

Once he knew where the mercury was, Perutz could calculate its wave contributions, including phase. Every spot of the mercury-treated crystal was a combination of the protein’s wave and the mercury’s wave, and he now knew the latter. So, if a spot had gotten darker compared to the plain crystal, then the protein’s wave must be in step with the mercury’s; if it had gotten fainter, the two must be opposed. For the spots that looked straight down the molecule’s symmetry axis, crest-to-crest (fully in step) and crest-to-trough (fully opposed) were the only two possibilities, so whether a spot darkened or faded gave the protein’s phase outright. In other words, they could be signed: plus for in step, and minus for opposed.

The phase values given by these signed waves matched the ones from the drying-and-salt experiments, confirming the results. “Everything checked and double-checked perfectly,” Bragg wrote later; “it was a thrilling time.”

With the heights and phases of these select spots, Bragg and Perutz could finally carry out a Fourier synthesis and add the waves into an electron density map, which shows where the molecule’s electrons crowd. It was the first map of a protein built from truly measured phases, rather than guessed. Because it only used spots looking down one axis, it showed the whole molecule flattened onto a single plane, all its depth squashed together. [The paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0205) reported that “neither polypeptide chains nor haem groups can as yet be recognized,” though it did settle one old question: there were “no regularly spaced layers,” and so no four sheets. Perutz called the image “entirely enigmatic”; Bragg likened it to “the last camp before the summit.”

The method worked, but to see the molecule’s full three-dimensional shape, Perutz needed the phases for all the other spots too, and for those, there was no symmetry to narrow the choices to in-step or opposed (plus or minus). For those, a phase could take any value in between, and the change in darkness would suggest *how far* the protein’s wave crest sat from the mercury’s, but not whether it was ahead or behind. That left two phase candidates for each spot.

In the spring of 1954, while Perutz was puzzling over his flat maps, Dorothy Hodgkin, née Crowfoot, stopped by the lab. “If you could get two heavy-atom derivatives,” she told him, “you could determine the structure in three dimensions.” The first derivative — a crystal with a heavy atom attached — was mercury<Fn>The mercury crystals counted as one derivative even though each molecule carried two mercury atoms, because the two sat at matching places on the two halves and gave a single comparison for each spot.</Fn>. A second derivative, with the atom in a different place, would provide another comparison and settle the phase ambiguity: the correct phase had to agree with both.

Then, almost immediately, Perutz hit roadblocks. The next batch of mercury crystals packed differently, so their films could no longer be compared spot by spot with the plain ones<Fn>The new crystals’ unit cell came out with one edge twice as long as before. Hemoglobin could pack into two different lattices depending on the acidity of the solution, and Perutz had been growing his crystals at almost exactly the point where it flipped. “Once I had the idea,” he said, “I of course solved it in an afternoon.”</Fn>. Then, in the autumn of 1954, he fell seriously ill, and as the illness stretched into late summer 1955, he thought he would have to resign, until a colleague suggested celiac disease and a diet without gluten brought him back part-time.

Meanwhile, since 1948, Kendrew had been working on myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen in muscle, and while Perutz was ill, he pushed ahead with isomorphous replacement. When Perutz returned, he found Kendrew “going ahead splendidly,” he said later, “and I—after having produced the method—was getting absolutely nowhere!”

</Wrap>

Myoglobin has just a single heme and about a quarter of hemoglobin’s size, and Kendrew had found excellent crystals of it from sperm whale<Fn>Kendrew’s horse myoglobin crystals had never grown past fine needles, too small for detailed x-ray work. Sperm whale myoglobin gave far better ones, and further batches of the meat were shipped to him from Lima, Peru, where it was still sold in the market.</Fn>. The mercury trick, however, did not work on myoglobin, which had no free cysteines for the mercury to bond to. Howard Dintzis, a biophysicist who had recently joined the team, set up racks of small bottles, each holding crystals of hemoglobin or myoglobin soaked in a different compound. Weeks later he photographed them, looking for what had made mercury work: spots in the same places, but darker or fainter. Once a compound passed this test, its position was found using the same Patterson method that Perutz used for mercury. By the middle of 1955, myoglobin had five heavy-atom derivatives.

Kendrew now had what Hodgkin had described: enough derivatives to pin down the phase of every spot, in every layer. But collecting this data for the three-dimensional map was a far bigger job than the flat one. Each photograph recorded only one layer of the crystal, so the crystal had to be rotated in small increments and photographed again, layer after layer, until all the spots had been recorded. This had to be done for the plain crystal as well as each of the derivatives<Fn>The photographs looking down the crystal’s axis showed where each heavy atom sat across the crystal, but not how deep it sat along that axis. Along it, the crystal has no fixed point, so no single heavy atom’s depth can be measured; only how far apart two heavy atoms sit in depth, which was all the two-derivative method needed. Perutz worked out one way to find it but fell ill before publishing: “This method was just waiting in my notebook.” David Harker in Brooklyn and Bragg each proposed others, and Kendrew drew on all three.</Fn>.

To keep the work manageable, Kendrew planned to work first at a resolution of six ångströms, or six tenths of a nanometer—features closer than that would blur—which was enough to separate neighboring stretches of the chain, but not to show individual atoms. This required just using the inner spots on the film, about 400 per crystal. Instead of grading spots by eye, his team used a densitometer, and the waves were added up on the university’s new EDSAC computer.

While Kendrew worked on the map, several hundred of Dintzis’s hemoglobin bottles sat unexamined. Before returning to America in 1956, he had blocked hemoglobin’s two cysteines, forcing mercury to settle somewhere else. He never looked at the results. Over the winter, Perutz’s research assistant, Ann Cullis, went through the bottles, and one showed “intensity differences of a kind we had not seen before,” as Perutz put it. The mercury had bound in a new place; hemoglobin had its second derivative. That spring, back at work full time, Perutz also traced the new packing to the acidity of the solution. In the space of a few months, the two problems that had held back hemoglobin since 1954 were both gone.

In the summer of 1957, after two years of work, Kendrew obtained a six-ångström map of sperm whale myoglobin, the first protein structure ever solved. There were 16 contour maps, each a slice through the unit cell, which he traced onto sheets of clear plastic and bolted together into a foot-long stack, so the cloud of electrons could be viewed from any angle. He used it to build a [plasticine model](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co13543/kendrews-original-model-of-the-myoglobin-molecule-molecular-models-proteins), but was himself disappointed with the model’s ugliness. Next door, after Crick and James Watson had solved the structure of DNA, Watson said the double helix was so pretty it had to exist. Kendrew’s model, meanwhile, reminded people of “abdominal viscera.” One crystallographer summed it up succinctly: “a horrible object, but beautiful work.”<Fn>Both reactions are recorded in Soraya de Chadarevian, “John Kendrew and myoglobin: Protein structure determination in the 1950s,” *Protein Science 27* (2018): 1136–43. The second is from an unnamed fellow crystallographer reacting to the photograph in *Nature*.</Fn>

Pretty or not, parts of the molecule were now recognizable. One was the heme, a flat disk of electron density held to at least four neighboring stretches of chain, with one face left open to oxygen. The other was the chain itself. At six ångströms, a tightly packed helical segment looked like a solid rod, and the straight runs all fitted the alpha helix, a coil the American chemist Linus Pauling had proposed in 1951. “The big surprise was that it was so irregular,” Kendrew said later. Crick had also been right in 1951: the chain ran straight for a stretch, then turned a corner, wandering through the molecule with no symmetry and no repeating pattern<Fn>Kendrew’s “big surprise” is from his interview with Judson (*The Eighth Day of Creation*, p. 539).</Fn>. When Kendrew and his colleagues [published](https://www.nature.com/articles/181662a0) their map in *Nature* in March 1958, they wrote that the arrangement was “more complicated than has been predicated by any theory of protein structure.”

For hemoglobin, Perutz followed the same path on a larger scale. It was four times the size of myoglobin and a less willing host for heavy atoms, and it took two more years to reach the six derivatives it needed<Fn>Two derivatives were the minimum in principle, but some heavy atoms barely changed certain spots, so extra derivatives were needed to fill in the gaps. That’s why hemoglobin needed six.</Fn>.

At the end of August 1959, he got his map, at a resolution of five and a half ångströms. It had taken more than a thousand spots, each measured in the plain crystals and again in all six derivatives, and the sums had run on EDSAC 2, one of the world’s fastest computers at the time. Twenty-two years after Haurowitz had told him to work out the structure of hemoglobin, Perutz finally had a shape. He built a model from it, slice by slice, and for the first time could see the molecule whose spots he had been photographing since 1937. It had four chains, two of one kind and two of another, packed into a compact ball. Every chain was folded almost the same way as Kendrew’s myoglobin; somehow, a protein from a horse’s blood had the shape of one from a whale’s muscle. And the four hemes sat in separate pockets on the surface, far apart, “perhaps the greatest surprise the structure presented to us,” Perutz [said](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/lecture/), because it had been assumed hemes sat close together in order to take up oxygen.

At the same time, Kendrew pushed myoglobin from six ångströms to two. That meant measuring the faint spots far beyond the inner circle, about a quarter of a million across all his crystals. The calculation barely fit in the memory of EDSAC 2. At two ångströms, the chain could be followed one amino acid at a time. The straight runs that had *looked* like Pauling’s alpha helix in 1957 were now proved, and the heme stood out sharply, its iron atom held to the chain by a single amino acid, a histidine<Fn>Kendrew called this “a striking confirmation” of suggestions made as much as thirty years earlier. James Conant had proposed in 1933 that the iron was bonded to the protein, and Jeffries Wyman, then Charles Coryell and Linus Pauling, argued that the bond was to a histidine</Fn>.

### Success

On 13 February 1960, Perutz’s [5.5 Å hemoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185416a0) and Kendrew’s [2 Å myoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185422a0) were published back to back in *Nature*, twenty-six years after Bernal had wandered the streets of Cambridge wondering how much the first protein photographs might one day reveal. In December 1962, Perutz and Kendrew received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Bernal wrote to congratulate Perutz: what he and Kendrew had done was “only the first swallow but what a summer it [was] going to be.”<Fn>Quoted in Georgina Ferry, *Max Perutz and the Secret of Life* (2007), p. 190.</Fn>

Soon after, isomorphous replacement enabled others to solve more protein structures, including [lysozyme](https://doi.org/10.1038/206757a0) in 1965 and [insulin](https://doi.org/10.1038/224491a0) in 1969. For the next three decades, it remained the standard way to find phases.

Perutz kept working on hemoglobin. In 1968, he published the structure of [horse oxyhemoglobin at 2.8 Å](https://www.nature.com/articles/219029a0), and by 1970 he had a map of hemoglobin without oxygen at the same detail. His aim, as he said at the [Nobel banquet](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/speech/), was to “explain the physiology of breathing in terms of the architecture of the haemoglobin molecule,” which his 1959 map had left unexplained.

Comparing those two, Perutz finally [saw](https://doi.org/10.1038/228726a0) how the molecule worked. Without oxygen, each iron sat slightly above the plane of its heme. When oxygen bound, the iron moved into the plane, and since it was bonded to its histidine, it pulled the histidine and its helix along. “When it clicks in and out of place it takes something with it,” he said. Without oxygen, a few weak links held the molecule’s two halves in a form that gripped oxygen loosely. Each oxygen that bound broke some of those links, and once enough had broken, the molecule switched to a form that took up oxygen readily. That was the cooperation Adair had measured a decade before handing Perutz his first crystals. The hemes acted on one another through the protein, which was why they could sit so far apart.

---

For three decades, scientists kept solving structures by isomorphous replacement, each taking several years. As the archive grew, a shortcut became possible. Proteins come in families, and relatives often fold in nearly the same way, as hemoglobin and myoglobin had shown. So, a new protein could use a solved relative’s phases to start and refine from there; no heavy atoms needed. By 1991, the archive held [about 700 structures](https://www.rcsb.org/statistics/growth/overall-structures), enough for this method, [molecular replacement](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0108767389009815), to become the [dominant route](https://journals.iucr.org/m/issues/2023/05/00/be5292/index.html) to determining new structures<Fn>Michael Rossmann and David Blow, both in Perutz’s group, laid the foundations for the method in 1962.</Fn>.

Meanwhile, new technologies sped up the work for proteins with no solved relative. Recombinant DNA let labs produce proteins in larger amounts; synchrotrons cut diffraction measurements to minutes; and software took over the calculations. A newer method, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), avoids crystals altogether by freezing a thin film of proteins and photographing them directly<Fn>Another method, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), avoids crystals too. It uses powerful magnets to measure the distances between pairs of atoms in a protein dissolved in water, then calculates a structure that fits them. First used successfully in 1985, it works best on small proteins and accounted for 1.3% of the structures deposited in 2025.</Fn>. Improved by new cameras in 2013, it produced 45.3% of the [nearly 21,000 structures deposited](https://cdn.rcsb.org/rcsb-pdb/general_information/news_publications/newsletters/2026q1/deposit.html) in the PDB in 2025, against 53.1% from x-ray crystallography.

AlphaFold, trained on this archive, effectively adds to it: its predicted structures can often stand in for the relative that molecular replacement needs. A [2024 study](https://journals.iucr.org/d/issues/2024/11/00/qu5006/) of recent structures solved via heavy atoms found that 97% could have been solved from a prediction instead. In cryo-EM, [neural nets](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07215-4) now build atomic models about as well as human experts, in [hours rather than months](https://mrclmb.ac.uk/news-events/articles/modelangelo-software-expands-cryo-em-toolkit-with-faster-atomic-model-building-and-identification-of-novel-proteins/).

More recently, agentic AI has begun connecting all these tools. In November 2026, [Anthropic described](https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-vault-particles-with-claude) a project in which Claude, its AI model, working with scientists, helped determine the structure of a vault particle, a hollow barrel of protein. With direction and occasional corrections, it searched protein databases for relatives of vaults, designed the DNA to produce them, optimized the purification method, and processed the cryo-EM data. The team estimated that purification went from 3 days to 90 minutes, and the first model from weeks to 34 hours<Fn>The timings are Anthropic’s own estimates. Early in the cryo-EM processing, Claude skipped a standard step and produced a map blended with its mirror image, which the researchers caught.</Fn>.

The vault’s structure is now uncovered, but its function is still unknown. This was where Perutz was in 1959: he had hemoglobin’s shape, but could not explain how it took up oxygen. That took another eleven years and a second structure. AI tools can predict a protein’s shape because of a large archive of measurements. What might we have to measure now for future tools to predict what a protein does?
