[AlphaFold’s database](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) holds more than 200 million predicted protein structures, one for nearly every protein whose sequence has been recorded. [why is it useful]. Every one was made by a model trained on a far smaller archive: the Protein Data Bank (PDB), which holds a little over 250,000 structures, each measured in a lab, often taking years of work on a single protein.

When the PDB opened in 1971, it included [just seven structures](https://www.rcsb.org/pages/about-us/history). Among the earliest were myoglobin and hemoglobin, solved in Cambridge in 1957 and 1959 by John Kendrew and Max Perutz, the first protein structures ever determined. [About 80%](https://rcsb.org/statistics/growth/experimental-method) of the quarter million structures measured since were solved by x-ray crystallography, tracing back to the method Perutz’s group worked out.

In 1937, when Perutz took his first photographs of hemoglobin crystals, he was where Lupas would be eight decades later: he had his diffraction data as spots on film, but no way to turn them into a picture of the molecule. No one was sure whether the structure of a protein could be measured at all. And certainly, he didn’t expect it to take sixteen years to find the method, and twenty-two to see the molecule.

Along the way, as Perutz was trying to solve hemoglobin's structure, there came war, exile, years of misleading results, and a younger colleague who told him that the model he was so proud of was wrong, until a letter from a stranger showed him the way. This is the story of those twenty-two years.

### X-Rays

Max Perutz joined J.D. Bernal’s lab in Cambridge by chance. He had asked a chemistry professor who was traveling there to put in a word for him at the biochemistry lab, but the professor came back with a place in x-ray crystallography instead. When Perutz protested that he knew nothing of crystallography, the professor told him, “You’ll learn it, my boy.”

Bernal had just begun moving x-ray crystallography from graphite and bronze into the realm of biology. In 1934, he and his research student, Dorothy Crowfoot, [produced](https://www.nature.com/articles/133794b0) the first x-ray diffraction photograph of a protein crystal, pepsin, overturning the widely held belief that proteins would not diffract at all. Crowfoot later recalled that on the night of the first photographs, Bernal “wandered about the streets of Cambridge, thinking of the future and of how much it might be possible to know about the structure of proteins if the photographs… could be interpreted in every detail.”

When Perutz arrived at Bernal’s lab, a small, dingy sub-department in the Cavendish, Bernal didn’t have any protein crystals left to give him to study. He instead made him work on rhodonite, a mineral found in blast-furnace slag. “Nasty minerals” were not what Perutz had come to study, but he used them to learn the techniques of x-ray crystallography.

The technique works by shining a narrow beam of x-rays through a crystal and recording, on photographic film placed behind it, the pattern of spots the crystal scatters them into, an effect [discovered](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19133461004) in 1912. In principle, from where the spots fall (their pattern) and how dark they are (their intensities), one can work back to where the atoms sit inside the crystal. By the time Perutz arrived in Cambridge, the method had been used to determine the structures of minerals, metals, and organic molecules of a few dozen atoms — but certainly nothing as large as a protein.

Spots form because x-rays are waves, and waves can combine. The x-rays used in crystallography have wavelengths of about a tenth of a nanometer, or one ångström (Å), which is roughly the distance between neighboring atoms in a molecule. Like other waves, they have amplitude (height, which relates to brightness) and phase, which describes where along its cycle a wave is (or where its crests fall). When waves are added, those in step reinforce each other, and those opposed cancel each other out.

When x-rays strike a molecule, its electrons send out new waves of their own, in every direction. A single molecule scatters far too little to record. But in a crystal, trillions of identical copies are stacked like bricks in every direction. Familiar crystals like salt, sugar and quartz are built this way, and under the right conditions, proteins can form crystals too. Each brick, in this instance — the small box holding one or a few molecules — is called the ‘<Term k="unitCell">unit cell</Term>.’

The spots on the film record two kinds of information. The pattern they create comes from the spacing *between* the unit cells. The waves from all the copies line up only in certain directions; wherever they do, they make a spot, and everywhere else they cancel. How dark the spots are depends on the arrangement of atoms *within* each unit cell. Because those atoms sit in different places, their waves combine differently in each direction: when they mostly reinforce each other, the spot is dark, and when they partly cancel, the spot is faint. Thus, the positions of the spots measure the unit cell, and the darkness of the spots is a coded picture of the molecule inside.

### The Big Idea

Fed up with blast-furnace slag, Perutz began thinking about using x-ray crystallography to solve parts of proteins. In the summer of 1937, while visiting Prague, he raised this idea with his cousin’s husband, a young chemistry professor named Felix Haurowitz. Perutz was considering taking on hemin, the chloride salt of the heme group found in hemoglobin, but Haurowitz talked him out of it and told him to go for the whole hemoglobin protein instead. Haurowitz even suggested a physiologist in Cambridge, named Gilbert Adair, who could provide hemoglobin crystals.

When Perutz got back to Cambridge, he approached Adair, who grew him crystals of horse hemoglobin while apologizing that they were “probably much too small” as he handed them over. They turned out to be excellent for x-ray work<Fn>A decade earlier, Adair had shown that hemoglobin had four heme groups with four iron atoms. He also established the equation that described how those four hemes took on oxygen cooperatively: the first reluctantly and each of the others more readily.</Fn>.

Crystals of chymotrypsin, a digestive enzyme, had also been assigned to Perutz, but they gave diffraction patterns he could not read, and so hemoglobin became, by elimination, his sole focus<Fn>The chymotrypsin crystals had arrived from the Rockefeller Institute. They were twinned — two lattices overlapping one another — which meant that after diffraction, there was no way to tell which spots belonged to which orientation. Adair’s hemoglobin, on the other hand, gave clean single crystals.</Fn>.

That winter of 1937-38, Max Perutz got his first encouraging results. The photographs of Adair’s wet horse methemoglobin crystals — a stable, oxidized form of hemoglobin — gave sharp spots right up to the edge of the film. This meant that every hemoglobin molecule in the crystal was identical to every other, down to the positions of their atoms. From the spacing of the spots near the center of the film, he measured the unit cell, and from the symmetry of the pattern, he showed that each molecule was made of two identical halves<Fn>From the cell’s volume and the crystal’s density, Perutz worked out that each cell held two hemoglobin molecules and that the molecule weighed 69,000, close to the figures Adair and Svedberg had measured by other methods (about 67,000 and 66,800).</Fn>.

</Wrap>

Perutz published these results in [a letter to *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/141523a0) on 19 March 1938, but was still a long way from actually solving the structure<Fn>Together with Bernal and Isidor Fankuchen, an American crystallographer.</Fn>. The issue, at the time, was that the film photograph preserves only part of the information needed to reconstruct the molecule. Each spot’s darkness told Perutz how strong a scattered x-ray wave was, but did not reveal the wave’s phase — where its crests and troughs lined up relative to those of the other waves. These phases are critical because two waves of equal strength can produce completely different results when added together. When aligned crest to crest, they reinforce one another and, when aligned crest to trough, they cancel.

To work backward from the spots to a picture of the molecule, then, Perutz needed to combine the waves mathematically. He knew their strengths, but without their phases, he did not know how to line them up. Different choices could produce different pictures from the same spots. For smaller molecules, crystallographers could guess an arrangement of atoms, calculate the spots it would produce, and compare them with the film. For a molecule with thousands of atoms, there were far too many arrangements to try. Finding the missing phases was the “phase problem,” and it held up the structure of hemoglobin for the next fifteen years.

The week before the *Nature* letter appeared, the world changed. On 12 March, Hitler annexed Austria. German troops marched into Vienna, and Perutz’s parents, Jewish by descent, fled to Prague. In an instant, Max Perutz had become a refugee<Fn>To make matters worse, his parents would no longer be able to fund his Cambridge education. Amid this turmoil, Perutz found a clever way to spend his summers in the mountains. A skier and mountaineer since childhood, he joined a field expedition to the Swiss Alps to study how snow turned into glacier ice. His glacier work was published the following year, and gave him a reputation as an expert on glaciers, one that would come in useful later.</Fn>.

Back in Cambridge that autumn of 1938, there was more change awaiting Perutz. Lawrence Bragg, then the leading figure in x-ray crystallography, had just been appointed head of the Cavendish, and Perutz’s supervisor, Bernal, had moved to Birkbeck College in London, “taking all of biological crystallography with him except Perutz”<Fn>Bernal was blocked from the Cambridge chair partly by his Communist Party membership.</Fn>.

Perutz was initially nervous to show Bragg, his new boss, what he had been working on. But when he finally mustered the courage weeks later, he won him over immediately. Perutz had taken “the best photographs of a protein at that time,” Bragg said, and they had “fired” his enthusiasm<Fn>Recorded in an interview with Horace Freeland Judson.</Fn>. Without delay, Bragg contacted the Rockefeller Foundation and secured a grant to support Perutz’s research<Fn>The position also let Perutz support his parents and bring them to England; they had fled from Prague to Switzerland after Czechoslovakia came under German pressure.</Fn>.

In January 1939, Bernal gave [a lecture](https://doi.org/10.1038/143663a0) focused on the phase problem. Direct analysis of x-ray diffraction photographs, he said, “is rendered impossible by the fact that we can never know the phases of the reflections corresponding to the different spots.” He speculated on two ways to solve this problem. First, perhaps it would be possible to recover some of the missing phase information by watching how the diffraction pattern changed as the crystal dried. Protein crystals contain water between their molecules. As that water leaves, the crystal can shrink, bringing the molecules closer together and changing how their scattered waves reinforce or cancel one another. If the molecules themselves kept their shape, comparing photographs at different stages of drying might help narrow down which phases could account for the changing intensities.

The second idea, which Bernal only briefly mentioned, was to add a heavy atom to the molecule<Fn>The heavy-atom idea was not new. Three years earlier, J. Monteath Robertson had worked out the complete structure of phthalocyanine, a blue dye of fifty-eight atoms, by comparing two crystals of it, one with a nickel atom at the center of the molecule and one without. The nickel changed how dark the spots were, and from those changes Robertson could work out where every atom sat.</Fn>. Such an atom, with its many electrons, scatters x-rays strongly. If its position in the crystal was known, then the waves it contributed could be calculated, including their phases. By measuring how the added atom made particular spots brighter or dimmer, researchers might infer how its waves lined up with the protein’s.

Perutz decided to try the first path. But then, around the same time, in September 1939, Britain declared war on Germany. A tribunal classified Perutz as a refugee and allowed him to continue his hemoglobin work<Fn>Tribunals classified German and Austrian nationals as security risks, doubtful cases, or refugees from Nazi oppression.</Fn>. But in May 1940, just a few weeks after receiving his PhD, Perutz was arrested after the Home Office extended internment to men previously judged harmless.

His internment took him through camps in Britain to a troopship bound for Canada and a camp of wooden huts above Quebec City, where the internees slept, a hundred men to a room, double-bunked, behind barbed-wire windows. A campaign by his colleagues in Britain eventually secured his release, and in January 1941 he landed at Liverpool, was formally set free, and was told to register with the Cambridge police as an [enemy alien](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1985/08/12/enemy-alien).

### The Phase Problem

Back at Cambridge, Perutz returned to drying crystals. Rapid drying destroyed the fine detail in the diffraction patterns, so he sealed the crystal in a thin glass capillary, with a trace of its own mother liquor — the solution in which it had grown — at each end<Fn>He was by now growing his own hemoglobin crystals with the help of the Molteno Institute. The capillary was about a millimeter across, and the crystal sat in the vapor of the liquor</Fn>. This way, it could be dried over several days and held at each drying stage for weeks.

Perutz photographed the crystal at four stages and found that the shrinkage was lopsided. The crystal contracted almost entirely in one direction, so he concluded that the molecules were packed in layers with liquid between them, which grew closer and slid over one another as the water drained away<Fn>Given this geometry, he could rule out the elongated ellipsoid shape physical chemists had earlier proposed.</Fn>. He [published these results](https://doi.org/10.1038/149491a0) in *Nature* in May 1942. From the same photographs, he concluded that each molecule was flat, “a platelet 36 Å thick,” the beginning of a wrong turn that would take most of the decade to correct.

But drying changed two things at once. As the layers moved closer, the liquid between them also grew saltier, and salt, with its heavier atoms, scatters x-rays more strongly, so Perutz could not tell which change had made a spot darker or fainter.

In 1942, a new crop of crystals, prepared in pure water, gave him a comparison where only one thing changed. Their spots fell in the same places as before, but many were darker or fainter, because the water between the layers scattered more weakly. By soaking salt back in, a little at a time, Perutz could make the liquid scatter more strongly while leaving the protein alone.

Each spot now combined two waves, the protein's, which Perutz wanted to know, and the liquid's, which he could control. To read the protein's wave from a changed spot, he first had to know the liquid's, and he could work that out only for one column of spots running through the center of the film. Those spots recorded the crystal straight down through its layers, like a core cut through a layer cake, and in that core the liquid was simply a flat layer of known thickness and saltiness.

Because each molecule had two identical halves, the protein’s wave for each of these spots could only be in step with the liquid's or opposed to it, so its phase came down to a sign, plus or minus. As salt went in, a spot that darkened was in step, and one that faded was opposed. That gave Perutz the signs of the seven spots nearest the center.

</Wrap>

With a strength and a sign for each of the seven protein waves, Perutz could finally add them back together. Because these spots looked at the crystal only from top to bottom, all they could give was a single line: how much matter lay at each depth through a protein layer. The line had four bumps about nine ångströms apart, marking four depths inside the protein where matter was concentrated, and Perutz read them as four thin sheets of protein chain stacked on one another.

Perutz and a research assistant [published their results](https://doi.org/10.1038/151714a0) in *Nature* in June 1943. After six years, the first partial picture of the inside of the molecule was a single line graph with four bumps<Fn>The same note printed a rough two-dimensional map from an air-dried crystal, built from only four spots, which showed dumb-bell-shaped molecules. “Nevertheless, with all its shortcomings,” they wrote, “it is the first direct picture of a small protein molecule.”</Fn>. It was one-dimensional data for a three-dimensional structure, and four sheets were a guess that would turn out to be wrong.

In 1943, Perutz was sent as a glacier expert to an Allied wartime project<Fn>The project, Habakkuk, was to build an aircraft carrier out of wood-pulp-reinforced ice. The idea, from Geoffrey Pyke, a journalist and inventor, was to make a ship of pykrete — ice mixed with wood pulp — which was far tougher than plain ice. Perutz tested it in a refrigerated meat store beneath London’s Smithfield Market, hidden behind frozen carcasses, and found it slowly sagged unless kept below about −16 °C. Keeping a ship that cold would have taken so much steel that, as Perutz put it, the U.S. Navy decided “Habakkuk was a false prophet.” He told the story in his essay “Enemy Alien.”</Fn>. When he returned to Cambridge in 1944, he found that his two research assistants had spent his absence photographing hemoglobin crystals and grading their diffraction spots by eye<Fn>There was no instrument in the lab that could read darkness automatically, so a calibration strip was made by exposing film to an x-ray beam for a series of times, producing a row of about twenty spots that went from barely visible to nearly black. This eye-balling generally had an error of 20-25% for medium and strong spots and 30-40% for the weak ones.</Fn>. The salt method had given him phases for only one column. A picture of the molecule would need spots analyzed in every direction — thousands of them — and without their phases, he had no way to add their waves together into a map<Fn>When a researcher wanted to grade a spot on a real photograph, it was compared against the strip, and the number that most closely matched the spot’s darkness was recorded. They were also roughly related, so a spot that scored 60 was about twice as dark as that scored 30.</Fn>.

But there was another way. If only the intensities of the waves were used, and all the phases kept constant, then the resulting sum would not be a map of the atoms but a map of the distances *between* the atoms, called a Patterson map. Since it needed only intensities, Perutz could make one from his photographs alone.

To understand how a Patterson map works, picture a dark classroom. You can’t see where the children are sitting, but you have a pack of notes that tells you how far apart each pair is, and in what direction. For example, it might say “Sam is 1 step to the right of Ali” or “Brian is 2 steps in front of Mia.” Now, suppose you go to the whiteboard, mark a dot in the middle, and for each note walk the steps it describes and put a sticker where you land. If the children’s desks are laid out in neat rows, then lots of pairs would be “1 step to the right” or “2 steps forward,” so lots of notes send you to the same spots and the stickers pile up. If, however, the children were scattered randomly, then the stickers would land all over the board and no pattern would stand out.

A Patterson map is built the same way: each pair of atoms adds a peak, and the more pairs share a spacing, the taller the peak grows. Perutz’s first maps used the spots from only one view of the crystal, so they were flat, the whole molecule pressed onto one plane. They pinned down its outside, which Perutz published in February 1947 as a squat cylinder, 57 Å across and 34 Å high, “the fuzzy outline of the molecule.” But inside, thousands of atoms made millions of pairs, and their peaks piled into a tangle. Ten years after his first photographs, the inside was still only the guess of four sheets.

Partway through those years, early in 1946, Perutz got a new partner. John Kendrew, a physical chemist, had spent the war on radar and in Southeast Asia, where he kept running into Bernal, who talked to him about proteins and Perutz’s work. Kendrew came back to Cambridge wanting to work on proteins himself, and Perutz taught him everything he knew about crystallography<Fn>One day, Perutz ran into Joseph Barcroft, a physiologist, who reminded Perutz that fetal hemoglobin, which he was studying, behaved differently from adult hemoglobin, and proposed that Kendrew look for the structural basis for this difference through x-ray crystallography. He could even supply the fetal hemoglobin from sheep. With that, Kendrew’s thesis topic was settled.</Fn>.

Perutz, meanwhile, had already set out to make the Patterson map in three dimensions. Where the flat maps had squashed the molecule onto one plane, a three-dimensional map would spread it back out as a stack of slices, one for each depth. He called it the only way to make the whole diffraction pattern “at least potentially capable of being interpreted.” But the map would be readable only if the inside was orderly. Chains running side by side in parallel bundles or flat layers would repeat the same spacings thousands of times and pile up into straight rods, while an irregular tangle would leave a featureless, unintelligible blur. Every plausible model of a globular protein assumed the orderly kind<Fn>Astbury’s stacked disks for egg albumin and Pauling’s layers were the models that assumed a regular interior</Fn>. Perutz too expected rods, as further evidence of his stacked sheets.

Measuring the 7,000 or so spots the map needed was a task “whose length and tediousness it will be better not to describe,” Perutz wrote. Adding up their waves was beyond any machine in Cambridge, so the work went to a punched-card firm in London<Fn>They were sent to the Scientific Computing Institute in London, where they used a procedure Dorothy Crowfoot’s group in Oxford had devised for penicillin</Fn>. In the spring of 1948, the answer came back as 31 contour maps, one for each slice through the unit cell, which Perutz traced onto transparent sheets and stacked to see the peaks in three dimensions.

The rods were there. Several peaks stretched into long, straight ridges, all running the same way: one through the center of the map and six surrounding it. Perutz read them as protein chains lying side by side in four layers, matching the four sheets he had proposed in 1943<Fn>The repeat matched Astbury’s spacing in keratin – the protein found in wool and hair – suggesting that hemoglobin’s chains were folded the same way and feeding the assumption that proteins had a regular structure.</Fn>.

On 8 June 1948, Perutz [submitted his findings](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1949.0005) to the Royal Society. The ‘parallel rods’ structure, he wrote, was “plausible and consistent with all the known evidence, but proof for the time being” eluded him, and he ended the paper by wondering whether hemoglobin could be taken any further at all.

### A Glimmer of Hope

That same month, Perutz and Kendrew presented their work at a symposium on hemoglobin. They also wrote [a review](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.6422) about what x-ray crystallography could and could not do for large molecules. In it, they named the phase problem as “the fundamental difficulty of all X-ray analysis, … encountered in its most acute form in the analysis of very large molecules,” and explained the two tricks crystallographers had used to get around it for smaller molecules.

One was the heavy-atom method, which involved attaching to the molecule an atom so heavy that its wave outweighs those of all the light atoms around it, so that each spot takes on roughly the heavy atom’s phase. Once that atom is located, its phases can be calculated and used to compute a first rough map. The other was isomorphous replacement, which involved comparing two crystals identical except for one swapped atom. Their spots fall in the same places, and since the swapped atom’s wave can be calculated, whether each spot darkens or fades shows how the rest of the molecule’s wave lines up with it — the same logic as Perutz’s salt experiment.

They then ruled out both for proteins because of their size: “even the heaviest of heavy atoms would make a negligible contribution to the reflexions from so large a unit cell, so it would exert no control over the phases.” A hemoglobin molecule has about 5,000 atoms, with tens of thousands of electrons, so adding a mercury atom’s 80 electrons was not expected to make a difference. Perutz also knew that Dorothy Crowfoot had tried adding heavy atoms to insulin, a much smaller protein, and seen no detectable changes.

With phases out of reach, the Patterson was the only road left. They now tried guessing after all, though only the shape of the folded chain. Through 1949, Bragg, Perutz, and Kendrew proposed chain after chain and compared each one’s Patterson map with the measured one, but none stood out; their models, [they admitted](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1950.0142) in March 1950, had “no obvious advantages over their alternatives.” They had ignored a chemical fact the American chemist Linus Pauling had established years earlier, that the bonds joining amino acids cannot rotate freely, and Pauling built that constraint into the model he [published](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.37.4.205) the following year as the alpha helix<Fn>Bragg, many years later, called the 1950 paper “the most ill-planned and abortive” piece of work he had been involved in. W. L. Bragg, "First stages in the x-ray analysis of proteins," *Reports on Progress in Physics* 28 (1965): 1–14, p. 6</Fn>.

While the three were still fitting chains, in the autumn of 1949, a 33-year-old physicist named Francis Crick joined the Medical Research Council unit that Perutz and Kendrew ran at the Cavendish. He was new to biology, had never done crystallography, and learned it largely by arguing with the two of them.

Crick began by checking the assumption, held since 1943, that hemoglobin was built from layers of parallel chains. If Perutz’s model were right, he calculated, the rods in the 1948 map should have been far taller than they were. He concluded that the chains could not run straight and parallel for long; they could run straight for about a dozen amino acids before they had to turn a corner.

In 1951, Crick presented these findings to Bragg, Perutz, and Kendrew in a talk titled What Mad Pursuit, essentially telling them that their assumptions for the last near-decade were incorrect. As Perutz would put it many years later: “Here was this new fellow… [who] was telling us that what we were so proud of, was wrong.”

</Wrap>

With guesswork exhausted, Bragg and Perutz [concluded](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1952.0136) that for a molecule this complex, “a direct attack seems the only course which may lead to success,” and a direct attack needed firmer numbers. Using a Geiger counter, Perutz put the spots on an absolute scale, measuring what share of the x-ray beam each one caught instead of grading them by eye. The numbers showed how weak the spots were: in an average spot, the waves from a molecule’s tens of thousands of electrons added up to the wave of only a few hundred, since most of them cancelled. The same measurements gave [the molecule’s outline](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X52000721), a “rather modest” result, Bragg later wrote, but “we snatched at such small successes to keep ourselves in heart to carry on”<Fn>Bragg, "First stages in the x-ray analysis of proteins," pp. 7, 9.</Fn>. Yet everything inside the molecule was still in the dark, and the phase problem had not budged.

### Heavy Atoms

Early in 1953, a letter arrived at the Cavendish from Austen Riggs, a young biochemist at Harvard whom Perutz had never met. Riggs had read Perutz’s two short letters on sickle-cell hemoglobin and enclosed [a paper of his own](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.36.1.1)<Fn>The letters did contain a real finding. Without oxygen, sickle-cell hemoglobin dissolved only about a tenth as well as normal hemoglobin, though the two were equally soluble when carrying oxygen. That was why cells sickle in the tissues, where they give up their oxygen. Perutz also found that under polarized light, sickled cells looked as if they had crystals inside them. Pauling was annoyed, feeling the conclusions were already implicit in his own work.</Fn>. It was about the cooperation Adair had measured in the 1920s: the first of hemoglobin’s four hemes takes up oxygen reluctantly, and each of the others more readily, so a graph of oxygen taken up against oxygen pressure rises in an S-shape. Riggs wanted to know whether hemoglobin’s cysteines — amino acids that end in a sulphur atom — had a part in it. Mercury grips sulphur tightly, so he capped the cysteines with a mercury compound — though only two of the six took it up — and watched the curve<Fn>He took this to mean that two lay on the surface of the molecule where the reagent could reach them and four were buried inside. Riggs concluded that the sulphydryl groups play a large part in the mechanism of heme-heme interaction.</Fn>. The curve flattened, but it stayed S-shaped.

“I jumped when I saw that,” Perutz said years later, “because it was clear to me that if it left the biological properties intact, then it would also leave the structure intact.” If the protein kept its shape, it would pack into crystals just as before, with the mercury at the same place in every unit cell. Two crystals identical except for a heavy atom were exactly what isomorphous replacement needed, the method he and Kendrew had ruled out five years earlier.

Their objection had been that one atom among 5,000 could never outweigh the rest. That was still true, but it only mattered for the heavy-atom method. Isomorphous replacement needed the mercury only to make the spots measurably darker or fainter. Perutz’s Geiger-counter measurements of 1951 had already shown how weak the spots were: an average one added up to the wave of only a few hundred electrons. A mercury atom’s 80, scattering together from one point, would be a sizeable share of that. Riggs had shown that the first half of the experiment — putting a heavy atom on a protein without changing it — could be done.

Perutz could not use Riggs’s material, though, because human hemoglobin did not form crystals suited to x-ray work. The mercury would have to go onto horse hemoglobin, which has two reactive cysteines, one on each half of the molecule. On the x-ray photographs of the new crystals, the spots fell in exactly the same places as before, but many were visibly darker or fainter<Fn>A control crystal was also grown from the same protein without mercury which showed the normal pattern, so the differences could only come from the heavy atoms.</Fn>. Perutz developed the two films in the summer of 1953 and, as he later told it, ran up three floors to bring Bragg down to the darkroom to see them side by side. It was the first time a heavy atom had measurably changed the diffraction pattern of a protein.

Over the following months, Perutz turned the two films into [signs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0203), by the same logic he had used with salt in 1943. A Patterson map built from the differences between the two films showed where the mercury sat, so its wave in every spot could be calculated, and whether a spot darkened or faded gave the sign of the protein’s wave. In 1943 this had given signs for one column of spots; now it gave them for a whole flat layer. “Everything checked and double-checked perfectly,” Bragg wrote later; “it was a thrilling time.”

Bragg and Perutz summed the signed waves into an electron density map, which shows where the molecule’s electrons crowd, the first such map of a protein built from phases that had been measured rather than guessed. Because the signs came from a single layer of spots, the map was flat, like Perutz’s Patterson maps of the 1940s. [The paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0205) reported that “neither polypeptide chains nor haem groups can as yet be recognized,” though it did settle one old question: there were “no regularly spaced layers,” and so no four sheets. Perutz later called the image “entirely enigmatic.” Bragg likened it to “the last camp before the summit.”

</Wrap>

The method worked, but the map lacked depth. To get depth, the rest of the spots needed to be analyzed as well, and for those, plus or minus was no longer enough; a protein wave’s crests could fall anywhere relative to the mercury’s. In the spring of 1954, while Perutz was puzzling over his flat maps, Dorothy Hodgkin, née Crowfoot, stopped by the lab. “If you could get two heavy-atom derivatives,” she told him, “you could determine the structure in three dimensions.” With one derivative, like the mercury crystals, the change in a spot’s darkness showed how far the protein’s crests lay from the mercury’s, but not whether they were ahead or behind<Fn>Its two mercury atoms counted as one derivative, since they were added together at symmetry-related sites and gave a single comparison per spot</Fn>. A second, with a heavy atom in a different place, would add a second known wave, and only one possibility would fit both.

The path forward needed a second derivative, but almost immediately Perutz hit a roadblock: the next batch of mercury crystals packed differently, so their films could no longer be compared spot by spot with the plain ones<Fn>The unit cell came out with one of its edges twice as long as before.</Fn>. Then, in the autumn of 1954, he fell seriously ill, and as the illness stretched into late summer 1955, he thought he would have to resign. A colleague suggested celiac disease, a reaction to the gluten in wheat flour, and after Perutz removed that from his diet, he began to recover<Fn>Other foods turned out to affect him too, and for the rest of his life he stuck to a short diet of meat, fish and mild cheese, with a few vegetables.</Fn>. “There was Kendrew going ahead splendidly,” he said later, “and I — after having produced the method — was getting absolutely nowhere!”

Since 1948, Kendrew had been working on myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen in muscle, and while Perutz was ill, he pushed ahead with isomorphous replacement. Myoglobin was a simpler protein, with a single heme, and about a quarter of hemoglobin’s size. Unlike Perutz’s hemoglobin crystals, however, Kendrew’s horse myoglobin crystals never grew past fine needles, which were too small for detailed x-ray work. It wasn’t until late 1952 that Kendrew found a source — a stock of sperm whale left over from wartime trials — which gave “the most marvellous myoglobin crystals”<Fn>Further batches were shipped to him from Lima, Peru, where sperm whale meat was still sold in the market.</Fn>.

The mercury trick, however, did not work on myoglobin because it had no free cysteines for the mercury to bond to<Fn>Ingram tried some other routes, like using a compound that bonds to the heme iron, but those didn’t work either.</Fn>. Howard Dintzis, a biophysicist with heavy-atom expertise who had recently joined the team, began systematically searching for suitable compounds, setting up racks of small bottles, each holding crystals of hemoglobin or myoglobin soaked in a different compound. After a few weeks, he photographed them, looking for what had made mercury work: spots in the same places, but darker or fainter. By the middle of 1955, myoglobin had heavy atoms at five different places.

Kendrew now had what Hodgkin had described: enough derivatives to pin down the phase of every spot on the films, in every layer. Once he had worked out how deep each heavy atom sat, by comparing the derivatives against one another, he could see that the solution of myoglobin was inevitable<Fn>Several people worked on this problem at once. Perutz combined suggestions from Crick and William Cochran into a method, but fell ill before publishing it: "This method was just waiting in my notebook," he said. David Harker in Brooklyn and Bragg each proposed methods of their own. Kendrew drew on these and fixed the final positions with a calculation run on the computer.</Fn>. He planned to work first at a resolution of six angstroms, meaning that features closer together than that would blur. That would be enough to separate neighboring stretches of the chain, but not to show side chains or individual atoms. Instead of grading spots by eye, his team measured them with a densitometer, and the waves were added up on the EDSAC, the university’s new electronic computer<Fn>Kendrew had seen the densitometer used on tissue sections by a colleague in London and traveled there to use it after hours until the unit could buy its own.</Fn><Fn>Kendrew’s first research student, Hugh Huxley, complained in 1949 about a two-week hand calculation to his friend John Bennett in Maurice Wilkes’s EDSAC group. Bennett then wrote a program that did it in half an hour. Kendrew learned to program with Bennett’s help and the two published the first paper on crystallographic computing with a digital computer in 1952. Perutz, on the other hand, did not trust the machine and kept his calculations on Hollerith punched cards.</Fn>. By his estimate, the data collection and calculation for that first map could have been done in about two weeks. It took two years.

During those two years, several hundred of Dintzis’s hemoglobin bottles sat unexamined. Before returning to America in 1956, he had tried one more thing with them. Since mercury only ever bound to hemoglobin’s same two cysteines, he blocked those first, to force it to settle somewhere else. He never looked at the results. Over the winter, Perutz’s research assistant, Ann Cullis, went through the bottles one by one, and one showed “intensity differences of a kind we had not seen before,” as Perutz put it. The mercury had bound in a new place; hemoglobin had its second derivative. That spring, back at work full time, Perutz also traced the new packing to the acidity of the solution<Fn>Hemoglobin could pack into two different crystal lattices, and the switch between them depended on the acidity; Perutz had been growing his crystals at almost exactly the point where it flipped.</Fn>. “Once I had the idea,” he said, “I of course solved it in an afternoon.”

In the summer of 1957, Kendrew’s two years of work came through. He got his electron density map of sperm whale myoglobin at a resolution of six angstroms, the first protein structure ever solved. There were sixteen contour maps, each a slice through the unit cell, which he traced onto sheets of clear plastic and bolted together into a foot-long stack so he could look at the cloud of electrons from any angle. From it, he built a [plasticine model](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co13543/kendrews-original-model-of-the-myoglobin-molecule-molecular-models-proteins). He was disappointed with its ugliness. Next door, after Crick and James Watson had solved the structure of DNA, Watson said the double helix was so pretty it had to exist. Kendrew’s model reminded people of “abdominal viscera.” One crystallographer summed it up succinctly: “a horrible object, but beautiful work.”

Pretty or not, parts of the molecule were now recognizable. One was the heme, a flat disk of electron density held to at least four neighboring stretches of chain, with one face left open to oxygen. The other was the chain itself. At six angstroms, a tightly packed helical segment looked like a solid rod, and the straight runs all fitted Pauling’s alpha helix, though that could not yet be proved. “The big surprise was that it was so irregular,” Kendrew said later. Crick had been right in 1951: the chain ran straight for a stretch, then turned a corner, wandering through the molecule with no symmetry and no repeating pattern. When Kendrew and his colleagues [published](https://www.nature.com/articles/181662a0) their map in *Nature* in March 1958, they wrote that the arrangement was “more complicated than has been predicated by any theory of protein structure.”

To get the three-dimensional map for hemoglobin, Perutz followed the same path on a larger scale. Hemoglobin was four times the size of myoglobin and a less willing host for heavy atoms, and it took two more years to reach the six derivatives a molecule this size needed. Its intensities were also read on the densitometer, and the waves were added up on EDSAC 2, one of the fastest computers then built.

At the end of August 1959, Perutz got his map. Twenty-two years after Haurowitz had told him to work out the structure of hemoglobin, Perutz finally had a shape. The molecule had four chains, two of one kind and two of another, packed into a compact ball. The twofold axis he and Bernal had found in his first photographs ran through the center, making the two halves identical, each holding one chain of each kind. Every chain was folded almost the same way as Kendrew’s myoglobin; somehow, a protein from a horse’s blood had the shape of one from a whale’s muscle. And the four hemes sat in separate pockets on the surface, far apart. That, Perutz [said](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/lecture/), “was perhaps the greatest surprise the structure presented to us.” Since the hemes worked together to take up oxygen, it was assumed they would be close to one another. “As it stands,” he added, “the structure of oxyhaemoglobin leaves its physiological properties unexplained.”

At the same time, Kendrew pushed myoglobin from six angstroms to two. That meant measuring the faint spots far beyond the inner circle, about a quarter of a million of them across all his crystals. The calculation only just fit in the memory of the EDSAC 2. At two angstroms, the chain could be followed one amino acid at a time<Fn>The map helped confirm or correct the sequencing of myoglobin that was underway at the Rockefeller</Fn>. The straight runs that had looked like Pauling’s alpha helix in 1957 were now proved to be exactly that, and the heme stood out sharply, its iron atom held to the chain by a single amino acid, a histidine<Fn>Pauling had proposed the same link on chemical grounds thirty years earlier</Fn>.

### Success

On 13 February 1960, Perutz’s [5.5 Å hemoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185416a0) and Kendrew’s [2 Å myoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185422a0) were published back to back in *Nature*, twenty-six years after Bernal had wandered the streets of Cambridge wondering how much the first protein photographs might one day reveal. In December 1962, Perutz and Kendrew received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their discoveries. Bernal wrote to congratulate Perutz: what he and Kendrew had done was “only the first swallow but what a summer it [was] going to be”<Fn>Quoted in Georgina Ferry, *Max Perutz and the Secret of Life* (2007), p. 190</Fn>.

Soon after, their method of isomorphous replacement enabled many others to determine the structure of other proteins. The first enzyme structure, that of [lysozyme](https://doi.org/10.1038/206757a0), was solved in 1965; the structure of [chymotrypsin](https://doi.org/10.1038/214652a0) was solved in 1967; and Dorothy Hodgkin solved the [structure of insulin](https://doi.org/10.1038/224491a0) in 1969, a full 34 years after taking her first x-ray photo of it. For the next three decades, isomorphous replacement remained the standard way to find phases.

Perutz kept working on hemoglobin, as he would until his death in 2002. In 1968, he published the structure of [horse oxyhemoglobin at 2.8 Å](https://www.nature.com/articles/219029a0), and by 1970 he had a map of hemoglobin without oxygen at the same detail. His aim, as he had put it at the [Nobel banquet](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/speech/), was to “explain the physiology of breathing in terms of the architecture of the haemoglobin molecule,” which his 1959 map had left unexplained.

Comparing the two, Perutz finally [saw](https://doi.org/10.1038/228726a0) how the molecule worked. Without oxygen, each iron sat slightly above the plane of its heme. When oxygen bound, the iron moved into the plane, and since it was bonded to its histidine, it pulled the histidine and its helix along. “When it clicks in and out of place it takes something with it,” he said. Without oxygen, a few weak links held the molecule’s two halves in a form that gripped oxygen loosely. Each oxygen that bound broke some of those links, and once enough had broken, the molecule switched to a form that took up oxygen readily. That was the cooperation Adair had measured a decade before handing Perutz his first crystals: the first oxygen went on reluctantly, the rest easily. The hemes acted on one another through the protein, which was why they could sit so far apart.
