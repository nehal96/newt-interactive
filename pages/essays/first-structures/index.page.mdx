For ten years, Andrei Lupas’s lab at the Max Planck Institute in Tübingen had been unable to solve the structure of Af1503, a membrane receptor from an archaeon that lives in hot vents on the sea floor. They had crystals and they had x-ray diffraction data of those crystals, but whatever method they tried, they could not get a picture of the molecule.

In August 2020, Lupas was serving as an assessor for CASP, a competition in which anonymous groups predict the shape of proteins whose structures have been measured but not yet published. The predictions from group 427 did not look like anything seen before. As Nick Grishin, one of the other assessors, [characterized it](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8166336/), “either this group is close to solving the folding problem or they cheated somehow.” Lupas had a way to check, one that could not be faked. He entered Af1503 as a target. Group 427’s model gave him the structure [in half an hour](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.370.6521.1144).

Group 427 was DeepMind, and the program was AlphaFold2.

[AlphaFold’s database](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) now holds more than 200 million predicted protein structures, one for nearly every protein whose sequence has been recorded. The Protein Data Bank, an open database of experimentally determined structures, holds a little over 250,000. All 200 million predictions were made by a model that was trained on the PDB.

When the PDB opened in 1971, it included [just seven structures](https://www.rcsb.org/pages/about-us/history). Among the earliest were myoglobin and hemoglobin, solved in Cambridge in 1957 and 1959 by John Kendrew and Max Perutz, the first protein structures ever determined. [About 80%](https://rcsb.org/statistics/growth/experimental-method) of the quarter million structures measured since were solved by x-ray crystallography, tracing back to the method Perutz’s group worked out. In 1937, when Perutz took his first photographs of hemoglobin crystals, he was where Lupas would be eight decades later: he had the spots, but no way to turn them into a picture of the molecule. No one was sure whether the structure of a protein could be measured at all. And certainly, he didn’t expect it to take sixteen years to find the method, and twenty-two to see the molecule.

This is the story of those twenty-two years.

---

Max Perutz joined J.D. Bernal’s lab in Cambridge through a chance encounter. As an undergraduate student at the University of Vienna, Perutz had become interested in the new field of biochemistry. In 1934, he learned that vitamins were being investigated at Gowland Hopkins’s lab at Cambridge, and so decided that was where he should go.

The next summer, in 1935, Perutz learned that Hermann Mark, a chemistry professor, was traveling to Cambridge, and asked him to inquire about a position in Hopkins’s lab. Mark, to Perutz’s disappointment, forgot. He did however talk with J.D. Bernal, the head of crystallography, who said that he was looking for a research student to join his lab. Mark suggested that Perutz join Bernal instead, to which Perutz objected, saying he knew nothing about x-ray crystallography. “You’ll learn it, my boy”, Mark replied, as Perutz remembered it. Bernal accepted, and in September 1936, Max Perutz arrived at the Cavendish to uncover the structure of molecules<Fn>Max Perutz, interview with Horace Freeland Judson in *The Eighth Day of Creation: Makers of the Revolution in Biology* (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Press, 1996), p. 486</Fn>.

Through the eyes of Bernal, Perutz was joining at an exciting time. Known to his friends as Sage thanks to his wide-ranging intellect, J.D. Bernal had just begun moving x-ray crystallography from graphite and bronze into the realm of biology. In 1934, he and his research student, Dorothy Crowfoot, [produced](https://www.nature.com/articles/133794b0) the first x-ray diffraction photograph of a protein crystal, pepsin, overturning the widely held belief that proteins would not diffract at all. She later recalled that on the night of the first photographs, Bernal “wandered about the streets of Cambridge, thinking of the future and of how much it might be possible to know about the structure of proteins if the photographs… could be interpreted in every detail.”

When Perutz arrived at Bernal’s lab, a small, dingy sub-department in the Cavendish, Bernal didn’t have any protein crystals left to give him to study. He instead made him work on rhodonite, a mineral found in blast-furnace slag. “Nasty minerals” were not what Perutz had come to study, but he used the work to learn the techniques of x-ray crystallography.

### How x-ray crystallography works

By the time Perutz started learning it, x-ray crystallography had been around for just over two decades. The technique works by shining a narrow beam of x-rays through a crystal and recording, on photographic film behind it, the pattern of spots the crystal scatters them into, an effect Max von Laue in Munich had [discovered](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19133461004) in 1912. From where the spots fall and how dark they are, one can work back to where the atoms sit inside the crystal. The next year, William Lawrence Bragg, then a 23-year-old research student at Cambridge, used them to [work out the structure of common salt](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1913.0083), the first crystal structure ever solved. In the years since, the method had been used to determine the structures of minerals, metals, and organic molecules of a few dozen atoms.

Spots form because x-rays are waves, and waves can combine. The x-rays used in crystallography have wavelengths of about a tenth of a nanometer, or one ångström (Å), close to the distance between neighboring atoms. Like other waves, they have amplitude (height, which relates to brightness) and phase (the offset). When trying to add waves, the ones in step reinforce each other, and those opposing cancel each other out.

When an x-ray wave hits a single atom, it excites the atom's electrons, and each electron sends out a wave of its own, with the same wavelength. Together they spread out from the atom in every direction, far weaker than the wave that made them.

Now put two atoms side by side. Each sends out its own wave, and the two waves overlap on their way to the film. They start in step, but to reach most places on the film, one wave has to travel farther than the other. Where the extra distance is a whole wavelength, crests still meet crests, the waves add, and the film darkens. Where it is half a wavelength, crests meet troughs, the waves cancel, and the film stays clear. The brighter the x-rays that reach the film, the darker it turns.

A single molecule scatters far too little to record. But in a crystal, trillions of identical copies are stacked like bricks in every direction, so their waves add up in the same directions and the spots grow dark enough to see. Familiar crystals like salt, sugar, and quartz are built this way, and under the right conditions, proteins can form crystals too. Each brick in this instance, a small box holding one or a few molecules, is called the <Term k="unitCell">unit cell</Term>.

Seen face-on, looking along the beam, a vertical row of atoms leaves bands running horizontally across the film. A horizontal row, correspondingly, leaves bands running vertically. Put the two together into a flat grid of atoms, and the waves are in step only where a horizontal band crosses a vertical one, and thus only at that intersection does the film darken, as spots. If each point in the grid is a molecule rather than a single atom, the spots stay in the same places, but each one is a different darkness. A real crystal is three-dimensional, with its molecules repeating in depth as well, but the idea is the same: the film darkens only where the waves from every row line up.

</Wrap>

The spots carry two kinds of information. Where they fall depends only on the size and shape of the unit cell: shrink the box, and the spots spread apart, just as the bands did when the two atoms moved closer. How dark they are depends on what's inside the box. The atoms of each molecule scatter the same way the two atoms did, their waves adding up in some directions and cancelling in others, so each spot's darkness reflects how those atoms are arranged. Together, the spots relay a coded picture of the molecule.

---

In the summer of 1937, Max Perutz began pondering a more worthwhile problem to tackle. While visiting Prague, he brought up this question with his cousin’s husband, a young chemistry professor named Felix Haurowitz. Perutz was considering taking on hemin, the chloride salt of the heme group found in hemoglobin. Haurowitz talked him out of it and told him to go for the whole molecule instead. He did not, however, have any hemoglobin crystals of his own to provide, but he named a Cambridge physiologist, Gilbert Adair, as someone who could supply them.

When Perutz got back to Cambridge that autumn, he approached Adair with this request. The ever-smiling and generous Adair quickly grew him crystals of horse hemoglobin, apologizing that they were “probably much too small” as he handed them over. They turned out to be excellent for x-ray work. A decade earlier, Adair had shown that hemoglobin had four heme groups with four iron atoms. He also [established the equation](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(18)85018-9) that described how those four hemes took on oxygen cooperatively: the first reluctantly and each of the others more readily.

During this time, crystals of chymotrypsin, a digestive enzyme, arrived from the Rockefeller Institute’s laboratories in Princeton and were assigned to Perutz. He began working on both together, with “no particular preference.” As he worked on chymotrypsin though, he found that its crystals gave diffraction patterns he could not read. A crystal’s pattern can only be read if it is one lattice through and through, every molecule predictably spaced out. Chymotrypsin’s crystals were twinned, two lattices overlapping one another, which meant that after diffraction, there was no way to tell which spots belonged to which orientation. Adair’s hemoglobin, on the other hand, gave clean single crystals, and so hemoglobin became, by elimination, Perutz’s sole focus.

<Wrap>

That winter of 1937-38, Max Perutz got his first encouraging results. The photographs of Adair’s wet horse methemoglobin crystals gave sharp spots right up to the edge of the film. This meant that every hemoglobin molecule in the crystal was identical to every other, down to the positions of their atoms. From the spacing of the spots near the center of the film he measured the unit cell<Fn>From the cell’s volume and the crystal’s density, Perutz worked out that each cell held two hemoglobin molecules and that the molecule weighed 69,000, close to the figures Adair and Svedberg had measured by other methods (about 67,000 and 66,800).</Fn>. The symmetry of the photographs showed that each molecule was made of two identical halves.

Perutz, with Bernal and Isidor Fankuchen, an American crystallographer, published the results in [a letter to *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/141523a0) on 19 March 1938. The letter ended on an optimistic note. Their work so far showed the "wealth of new data" that x-rays could draw from a well-crystallized protein, and they called for the method to be applied to many more proteins. Drying, they wrote, "may make possible the direct Fourier analysis of the molecular structure once complete sets of reflections are available in different states of hydration." They also noted that the positions of the heme groups could not yet be inferred. What stood in the way was that the film photograph records the intensity of each spot (how dark it is), which comes from the amplitude of the wave, but it cannot record the wave’s phase, which is where along the cycle the wave arrives. The structure is rebuilt as a Fourier series, a sum of all the spots’ waves, and each wave has to go in with both its amplitude and its phase. Without phases there was no way to add the waves back together into a picture of the molecule. This was the phase problem, and it would hold them up for the next fifteen years.

</Wrap>

The week before the *Nature* letter appeared, the world changed. On 12 March, Hitler annexed Austria. German troops marched into Vienna, and Perutz’s parents, Jewish by descent, fled to Prague at once. In an instant, Max Perutz had become a refugee. To make matters worse, his parents would no longer be able to fund his Cambridge education, and so he would run out of money too.

Amid this turmoil, Perutz found a clever way to spend his summers in the mountains. A skier and mountaineer since childhood, he joined a field expedition to the Swiss Alps to study how snow turned into glacier ice. His glacier work was [published](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1939.0108) the following year, and gave him a reputation as an expert on glaciers, one that would come in useful later.

Back in Cambridge that autumn of 1938, there was more change awaiting Perutz. Bragg, by then the leading figure in x-ray crystallography, had just been appointed head of the Cavendish, taking over from Ernest Rutherford, who died the previous year. He was not a popular choice among the nuclear physicists that mostly made up the lab, but for Perutz he could be integral. After all, Perutz’s previous supervisor, J.D. Bernal, had moved to Birkbeck College in London in early 1938, “taking all of biological crystallography with him except Perutz”<Fn>Bernal was blocked from the Cambridge chair partly by his Communist Party membership.</Fn>.

Perutz, however, was nervous to show Bragg what he had been working on. When he finally mustered the courage weeks later, Bragg was won over immediately. Perutz, he told the writer Horace Judson decades later, had taken “the best photographs of a protein at that time”, and his enthusiasm had been “fired” by them. Without delay, he contacted the Rockefeller Foundation, requesting a grant to support Perutz’s research. It was secured within weeks, and the position let Perutz guarantee his parents’ support and secure their immigration to England<Fn>£275 per year for three years, and £100 for a more powerful x-ray tube</Fn><Fn>They had fled to Switzerland after Czechoslovakia came under German pressure</Fn>.

In January 1939, Bernal gave [a lecture](https://doi.org/10.1038/143663a0) that named the phase problem outright. Direct analysis of the photographs, he said, “is rendered impossible by the fact that we can never know the phases of the reflections corresponding to the different spots.” He set out two ways past it. The first was to watch how the intensities of the spots on the photograph changed as the crystal dried. The second, which he only mentioned, was to add a heavy atom. The heavy-atom idea was not new. Three years earlier, J. Monteath Robertson had [worked out the complete structure of phthalocyanine](https://doi.org/10.1039/JR9360001195), a blue dye of fifty-eight atoms, by comparing two crystals of it, one with a nickel atom at the center of the molecule and one without. The nickel changed how dark the spots were, and from those changes Robertson could work out where every atom sat. Whether the same could be done for a molecule the size of hemoglobin, with its five thousand atoms, was a different question. Perutz decided to try the first path.

On 3 September 1939, Britain declared war on Germany. Tribunals classified German and Austrian nationals as security risks, doubtful cases, or refugees from Nazi oppression. Perutz was classified as a refugee and allowed to continue his hemoglobin work. But then, in May 1940, just a few weeks after receiving his PhD for a thesis on the crystal structure of hemoglobin, Perutz was arrested. The Home Office had extended internment to men previously judged harmless.

His internment took him through camps in Britain to a troopship bound for Canada and a camp of wooden huts above Quebec City. There, a hundred men to a room, double-bunked, behind barbed-wire windows, he organized a university with the other interned academics. A campaign by his colleagues in Britain eventually secured his release, and in January 1941 he landed at Liverpool, was formally set free, and was told to register with the Cambridge police as an [enemy alien](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1985/08/12/enemy-alien).

Back at Cambridge, Bragg encouraged Perutz to continue his protein structure research. He returned to the drying problem where he last left off. Rapid drying destroyed the fine detail in the diffraction patterns, so Perutz set off on figuring out a way to slow the process. In his experiment, instead of letting the crystal dry in open air, he sealed it in a thin glass capillary, with a trace of its own mother liquor at each end<Fn>He was by now growing his own hemoglobin crystals with the help of the Molteno Institute. The capillary was about a millimeter across, and the crystal sat in the vapor of the liquor</Fn>. Inside the tube the crystal lost water only gradually, so that a complete drying now took several days rather than minutes.

Perutz recorded four stages this way: fully wet, two intermediate, and air dried. The two intermediate stages gave patterns nearly as detailed as the wet crystal. But he found that shrinkage was lopsided. The crystal contracted almost entirely in one direction, and so Perutz read this as molecules lying in rigid sheets, with the water between the sheets draining away and the sheets sliding over one another<Fn>Given this geometry, he could rule out the elongated ellipsoid shape physical chemists had earlier proposed.</Fn>. He [published these results](https://doi.org/10.1038/149491a0) in *Nature* in May 1942.

The sheet interpretation would turn out to be the beginning of a wrong turn, one that would take most of the decade to correct. But the experiment showed that hemoglobin crystals could be altered without wrecking them, while still giving sharp patterns. Every later method he would go on to try would rest on modifying a crystal and comparing the changes in x-ray patterns to discern clues about the structure.

The idea behind these comparisons was to change one part of the crystal and leave the rest alone. The change adds a known wave to every spot, and whether a spot then darkens or fades shows which way its own wave points. For the spots Perutz was using, the crystal’s symmetry left each wave only two choices, in step or half a wave out — a plus or a minus sign. Drying only half worked in this regard. Perutz could measure only a few drying stages, which narrowed the signs down to a handful of possibilities. He picked the one that gave a molecule of the right size and density, but to be sure, he needed a cleaner comparison: the liquid layer changing while the protein layers stayed where they were.

In 1942, a new crop of methemoglobin crystals gave him exactly that. Unlike the previous ones, these had been prepared in pure water, with no salt in them at all. When Perutz photographed them, he found that the molecules were packed identically, but the darkness of the spots was different. Only the liquid inside had changed. By swapping salty liquid for pure water, he had a way of changing the known wave without affecting the protein.

The comparison worked, and gave the same signs as drying, but within limits. Once Perutz had worked out the liquid’s wave, it gave him signs for seven spots (out of the thousands in total), all lying along one direction perpendicular to the protein layers. Adding those seven waves gave a graph of density of matter at each depth through the crystal. It would be like knowing how the weight of a lasagna changes as you move from the bottom of the dish to the top, but nothing about how it changes from side to side. The resulting graph had four bumps of about equal height, each spaced a little less than nine angstroms apart. Perutz took them to indicate four thin sheets of polypeptide chain stacked on one another, with side groups sticking up and down between the sheets.

</Wrap>

Perutz and his research assistant, Joy Boyes-Watson, [published their results](https://doi.org/10.1038/151714a0) in *Nature* in June 1943. After six years, the first partial picture of the inside of the molecule was a single line graph with four bumps<Fn>The same note printed a rough two-dimensional map from an air-dried crystal, built from only four spots, which showed dumb-bell-shaped molecules. “Nevertheless, with all its shortcomings,” they wrote, “it is the first direct picture of a small protein molecule.”</Fn>, which they guessed to be a flat, stacked sheet structure. It was one-dimensional data for a three-dimensional structure, and the guess would turn out to be wrong.

For most of 1943, Perutz was sent as a glacier expert to work on Habbakuk, an Allied wartime project to build an aircraft carrier out of wood-pulp-reinforced ice<Fn>The idea, from Geoffrey Pyke, a journalist and inventor, was to make a ship of pykrete — ice mixed with wood pulp — which was far tougher than plain ice. Perutz tested it in a refrigerated meat store beneath London’s Smithfield Market, hidden behind frozen carcasses, and found it slowly sagged unless kept below about −16 °C. Keeping a ship that cold would have taken so much steel that, as Perutz put it, the U.S. Navy decided “Habakkuk was a false prophet.” He told the story in his essay “Enemy Alien.”</Fn>. After that didn’t work out, in early 1944, Perutz returned to Cambridge. In his absence his two research assistants had been photographing hemoglobin crystals and grading their diffraction spots by eye<Fn>There was no instrument in the lab that could read darkness automatically, so a calibration strip was made by exposing film to an x-ray beam for a series of times, producing a row of about twenty spots that went from barely visible to nearly black. This eye-balling generally had an error of 20-25% for medium and strong spots and 30-40% for the weak ones.</Fn>. The seven signs from 1943 were enough for a line graph, but any picture of the molecule would need every spot on the film turned into a number, and there were thousands of them<Fn>When a researcher wanted to grade a spot on a real photograph, it was compared against the strip, and the number that most closely matched the spot’s darkness was recorded. They were also roughly related, so a spot that scored 60 was about twice as dark as that scored 30.</Fn>.

Perutz could not grade them all, so he settled on the few hundred that showed the molecule from one side, and had them graded in salt solutions and at different stages of drying. Over three years the tally ran to several thousand spots. Without phases, he could not add the waves back together into a picture of the molecule, but there was another way to add them. In 1934, the crystallographer Lindo Patterson [had shown](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.46.372) that if every wave goes in with its intensity alone, as though all the phases were the same, the sum is not a map of the atoms but a map of the distances between them. Wherever two atoms sit a certain distance apart, the map has a peak that same distance from its center, and the more often that distance occurs in the molecule, the stronger the peak, almost acting like a tally. Because the film records exactly these intensities, Perutz could make such a map without knowing a single phase. [His maps](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1947.0104) pinned down the molecule’s outside, but not its inside — inside, with thousands of atoms, every distance between them piled into a tangle of overlapping peaks.

In early 1946, a new face had joined the Cavendish. John Kendrew, a physical chemist whose doctoral research had been interrupted by the war, had worked on airborne radar and operational research before being posted in Southeast Asia. There, during the war, he had kept running into J.D. Bernal<Fn>J.D. Bernal was the scientific advisor to Mountbatten and was the one who suggested Perutz to the Habbakuk project.</Fn>. Kendrew would spend a good amount of time with Bernal, listening to him talk about x-ray crystallography, Max Perutz’s work, and its prospects of uncovering the structures of biological molecules. When he finally reached home, he had two choices: go to London to work with Bernal, or return to Cambridge and ask to join Perutz’s team. He had unused scholarship money in Cambridge, so back there he went.

At the Cavendish, Max Perutz quickly taught Kendrew the elements of crystallography that he had learned over the last nine years. Perutz also arranged a doctorate for Kendrew under Bragg, on the structural differences of fetal hemoglobin<Fn>One day, Perutz ran into Joseph Barcroft, a physiologist, who reminded Perutz that fetal hemoglobin, which he was studying, behaved differently from adult hemoglobin, and proposed that Kendrew look for the structural basis for this difference through x-ray crystallography. He could even supply the fetal hemoglobin from sheep. With that, Kendrew’s thesis topic was settled.</Fn>. Kendrew completed his thesis work in about a year. By the end of 1947 he and Perutz had written [a paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1948.0087) showing that fetal and adult hemoglobins crystallized differently and were therefore different proteins, but unable to say how the molecules differed. This was the last time Kendrew worked on hemoglobin. His doctorate, awarded in 1949, included this work alongside the beginnings of a protein of his own choosing: myoglobin, “the best next,” as he later put it.

In the spring of 1948 the results of the three-dimensional Patterson synthesis came back from London. The Patterson maps of 1947 had used a few hundred spots from the molecule as seen from one side, with everything inside it overlapping on the page. A three-dimensional map would separate what those projections had piled on top of one another, but it required grading all the spots in the pattern, some 7000 or so.

What Perutz wanted from this map was further evidence of the ‘stacked sheets’ model from 1943. If the chains inside the protein were an irregular tangle, the distances between atoms would occur about equally often in every direction, and the map would be a featureless, unintelligible blur. If, however, the chains ran side by side in parallel bundles or flat layers, the same distances would repeat thousands of times, and would pile up into straight rods on the map. Every plausible model of a globular protein was assumed to be the latter<Fn>Astbury’s stacked disks for egg albumin and Pauling’s layers were the models that assumed a regular interior</Fn>. Perutz too expected rods.

Making the map required a little over 400 million additions. No machine in Cambridge could do this calculation, so the numbers were sent to London, where an operator converted them into punch cards and ran them through Hollerith tabulators<Fn>They were sent to the Scientific Computing Institute in London, where they used a procedure Dorothy Crowfoot’s group in Oxford had devised for penicillin</Fn>. The answer came back as 31 contour maps, one for each slice through the cell, which Perutz traced onto transparent sheets and stacked so he could see depth.

When Perutz examined the stack, he found that the rods were there. One ran through the center with a repeat of 5 Å along it, and a ring of parallel rods surrounded it. He read these as folded chains lying side by side, stacked in four layers, confirming his proposal in 1943<Fn>The repeat matched Astbury’s spacing in keratin — the protein found in wool and hair — suggesting that hemoglobin’s chains were folded the same way and feeding the assumption that proteins had a regular structure.</Fn>. In May 1948, Linus Pauling, the Caltech chemist, visited Cambridge and saw these rods and suspected that they were folded in a helix shape he had recently formulated — later named the alpha helix — but kept it to himself.

On 8 June 1948, Perutz [submitted his findings](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1949.0005) to the *Royal Society*. He had doubts. He wondered if it was "too arbitrary to single out some features in the vector structure, however prominent, just because they lend themselves to interpretation and to neglect others because they do not," admitting that no satisfying answer could be given. The ‘parallel rods’ structure he was proposing, he wrote, was "plausible and consistent with all the known evidence, but proof for the time being eludes [him]." At the end of the paper, he asked whether the x-ray analysis of hemoglobin could be carried further at all, or whether it made more sense to focus on smaller proteins that others were beginning to study.

In June 1948, Perutz and Kendrew presented their work at a symposium in memory of Joseph Barcroft, the physiologist who had given Kendrew his thesis topic. They later wrote [a review](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.6422) about what x-ray crystallography could and could not do for large molecules. In it, they named the phase problem as “the fundamental difficulty of all X-ray analysis, … encountered in its most acute form in the analysis of very large molecules,” and explained the two tricks crystallographers had used to get around it for smaller molecules. One was the heavy-atom method, which involved attaching a heavy atom to the molecule, one which scatters far more strongly than the light atoms, finding where it sits, and using its phases in place of unknown ones to compute a first rough map. The other was isomorphous replacement, which involved comparing two crystals identical except for one swapped atom. Their spots fall in the same places but differ in darkness, and once the swapped atom’s position is known, those differences reveal the phases.

They then ruled out the possibility that either of these would work with proteins because proteins were too large: "even the heaviest of heavy atoms would make a negligible contribution to the reflexions from so large a unit cell, so it would exert no control over the phases." A hemoglobin molecule has about 5000 atoms, with tens of thousands of electrons, so one added mercury atom’s 80 electrons was expected not to make a difference. Perutz also knew that Dorothy Crowfoot had tried this technique on insulin, a much smaller protein, and seen no detectable changes. With phases out of reach, the Patterson was the only road left.

Ruling out the phases also meant that the only way forward was to work backwards. Instead of computing the structure from data, Bragg, Perutz, and Kendrew could propose structures and see which of them could have produced the data. Through 1949, they did exactly this. They proposed chains with the 5.1 Å repeat seen in keratin and in their own results, and tested each by comparing its Patterson map with the measured one. Several of the chains matched part of the data, but none matched it well enough to stand out from the rest. When the three [submitted their results](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1950.0142) to the *Royal Society* in March 1950, they admitted that their models had “no obvious advantages over their alternatives."

The paper’s shortcoming was that it treated the chain purely as a geometry puzzle to be fitted to x-ray spacings. It ignored a chemical fact Pauling had established years earlier, that the peptide bond cannot rotate freely, which rules out most of the chains they had drawn. Pauling built that constraint into his own model, and [published it](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.37.4.205) the following year as the alpha helix. Bragg, many years later, called the 1950 paper “the most ill-planned and abortive” piece of work he had been involved in<Fn>W. L. Bragg, ["First stages in the x-ray analysis of proteins,"](https://doi.org/10.1088/0034-4885/28/1/301) *Reports on Progress in Physics 28* (1965): 1–14, p. 6</Fn>.

While the three were still fitting chains, in the autumn of 1949, a 33-year-old physicist named Francis Crick joined the Cavendish. He was new to biology and had never done crystallography, but reading Schrödinger's *What is Life?* had convinced him to work on the structure of living molecules. He joined the Medical Research Council unit that Perutz and Kendrew ran at the Cavendish, as a research student of Bragg’s with Perutz as his supervisor, and learned crystallography largely by arguing with the two of them.

<Wrap>

Crick began by checking the assumption, held since 1943 and seemingly confirmed by the 1948 Patterson map, that hemoglobin was built from layers of parallel rods. The height of a Patterson peak counts how often a distance repeats, so parallel rods should give tall ones. When Crick worked backwards from Perutz’s parallel rod model, he got peaks that were much higher than anything in the actual data. The only conclusion he could draw was that hemoglobin’s chains could not run straight and parallel for long; a chain could run straight for about a dozen amino acids before it had to turn a corner. Hemoglobin was not built like the keratin or the others discovered so far. A globular protein, [he wrote](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X52001118), “may be more like a three-dimensional framework, and may need a perspective drawing to show its main features.”

Crick, a second-year student, presented these findings to Bragg, Perutz, and Kendrew, in a talk titled *What Mad Pursuit*, essentially telling them that their assumptions for the last near-decade were incorrect. As Perutz would put it many years later: “Here was this new fellow, whom you couldn't stop talking… [who] was telling us that what we were so proud of, was wrong."

</Wrap>

In November 1949, [Pauling’s group showed](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.110.2865.543) that sickle-cell hemoglobin and normal hemoglobin behaved as chemically different molecules. Perutz, despite not knowing the structure of a single hemoglobin, wanted to explore if there was a structural difference between the two. He found none — the x-ray patterns of the oxygenated crystals were identical — and wrote up the work in two short letters to *Nature* over [1950](https://doi.org/10.1038/166677a0) and [1951](https://doi.org/10.1038/167929a0)<Fn>The letters did contain a real finding. Without oxygen, sickle-cell hemoglobin dissolved only about a tenth as well as normal hemoglobin, though the two were equally soluble when carrying oxygen. That was why cells sickle in the tissues, where they give up their oxygen. Perutz also found that under polarized light, sickled cells looked as if they had crystals inside them. Pauling was annoyed, feeling the conclusions were already implicit in his own work.</Fn>. Publishing them would come to be fortuitous in an unexpected way in the years to come.

Over the course of 1951, Perutz made two improvements to the experiment. He used a Geiger counter to put the spot intensities on an absolute scale. He also turned the salt experiment of 1943 into a longer series, soaking the same crystal in steadily stronger solutions. Because salt fills the liquid between molecules but stays out of the protein, the spots that fade as salt rises are the ones carrying the molecule’s outline, and with the absolute intensities that fading could be turned into dimensions. Bragg and Perutz [published the result](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X52000721) in *Acta Crystallographica* in 1952, which described hydrated hemoglobin as a spheroid of roughly 55 by 55 by 65 Å, with a volume near 116,000 Å³.

Fourteen years after Perutz’s first hemoglobin patterns had given him the size of the unit cell, the group could now give the size and shape of the molecule within it. Bragg, writing years later, would call it a “rather modest result”, but also “the first definite quantitative piece of knowledge to be won.” “We snatched at such small successes to keep ourselves in heart to carry on,” he wrote of that result and others like it<Fn>Bragg, "First stages in the x-ray analysis of proteins," pp. 7, 9.</Fn>. But everything inside the molecule was still in the dark. The phase problem had not budged.

---

Early in 1953, a letter arrived at the Cavendish from Austen Riggs, a young biochemist at Harvard. Perutz had never met or corresponded with him before, but Riggs had read the two short Nature letters Perutz put out on sickle-cell hemoglobin and enclosed [a paper of his own](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.36.1.1). Riggs wanted to understand how hemoglobin’s four heme groups influenced one another’s affinity for oxygen, and whether the six cysteines in human hemoglobin had a part in it. Cysteine is the amino acid whose side chain ends in a sulphydryl group (a sulphur atom bonded to a hydrogen). Earlier work had shown that certain mercury compounds attach themselves to hemoglobin onto exactly those groups, because mercury binds sulphur far more tightly than anything else on the protein. His plan was to cap those sulphurs with a mercury compound, para-chloromercuribenzoate, and see whether the oxygen curve changed.

When Riggs applied the reagent, only two of the six cysteines took it up<Fn>He took this to mean that two lay on the surface of the molecule where the reagent could reach them and four were buried inside. Riggs concluded that the sulphydryl groups play a large part in the mechanism of heme-heme interaction.</Fn>. Blocking those two flattened the curve considerably; the hemes still cooperated, but weakly. He also ran the same experiment on sickle-cell hemoglobin and found no difference from the normal protein, and it was that comparison that prompted him to send his paper to Perutz.

"I jumped when I saw that," Perutz said years later, "because it was clear to me that if it left the biological properties intact, then it would also leave the structure intact." Riggs’s graph showed a flatter curve, but it was still sigmoid. Despite having two mercury atoms bolted onto its surface, hemoglobin could still bind oxygen cooperatively. And if the protein kept its shape, then it would pack into crystals the same way it did before, each molecule in the same position and orientation, with the mercury atom fixed at the same site in every unit cell. Comparing crystals with and without a heavy atom this way — isomorphous replacement — could then reveal the missing phases.

Five years earlier, Perutz and Kendrew had dismissed heavy-atom tricks for proteins, reasoning that one atom among five thousand would make no noticeable difference. Perutz’s absolute intensity measurements had since changed his mind. They showed that a hemoglobin crystal diffracts x-rays weakly, because the waves from most of its atoms cancel one another out. A mercury atom’s electrons sit at a single point and scatter together, so its wave would be large enough to darken or fade the spots by a measurable amount. That was all that isomorphous replacement needed; unlike the heavy-atom method, it did not require one atom to outweigh the whole molecule. Riggs’s paper had just shown that the first half of the experiment — placing a heavy atom on a protein without disturbing it — could be done.

Perutz could not use Riggs’s material: human hemoglobin did not crystallize in a form suited for x-ray analysis. The mercury would have to go onto horse hemoglobin. This task fell to Vernon Ingram, a chemist who had joined the group a few months earlier. Horse hemoglobin has two reactive cysteines, one on each half of the molecule, so each molecule picked up two mercury atoms. David Green, Perutz’s graduate student, then grew crystals from the mixture. Under the microscope, they looked like ordinary horse hemoglobin crystals, and on the x-ray photographs the unit cell dimensions came out nearly identical, but the intensities had changed. Many spots were visibly stronger or weaker than their counterparts in the native pattern<Fn>A control crystal was also grown from the same protein without mercury which showed the normal pattern, so the differences could only come from the heavy atoms.</Fn>. Perutz developed the two photographs in the summer of 1953 and, as he told it later, ran up three floors to bring Bragg down to the darkroom to look at them together. It was the first time a heavy atom had measurably changed the diffraction pattern of a protein.

Over the following months, Perutz turned the heavy-atom photographs into numbers. The first task was to find where in the unit cell the mercury sat, and a Patterson map built from the differences between the two sets of intensities revealed that. With the mercury positions fixed, its contribution to every spot — both amplitude and phase — could be computed outright. Then by comparing the two films spot by spot, some phase information could be calculated. This test yielded only a plus or minus sign however, which isn’t enough as phase information for most waves, which can be any angle. For a specific set of waves though, the ones that come from straight down hemoglobin’s axis of symmetry, just the plus or minus is enough. Perutz, Ingram, and Green reported the signs for more than two hundred such reflections in [a paper on ‘sign determination by isomorphous replacement’](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0203). “Everything checked and double-checked perfectly,” Bragg wrote years later; “it was a thrilling time.”

Bragg and Perutz summed the signed reflections into an electron density map, which shows where the molecule’s electrons crowd. Just as a hiking map draws lines of equal altitude, it draws lines of equal density. It was the first such map of a protein built from phases that had been measured rather than guessed. Because only the down-the-axis reflections carried phases, the sum could only be a projection — the density of the whole molecule, thirty to forty layers deep, squashed onto a single plane as if every atom had been pressed flat onto the page. [The paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0205) reported that “neither polypeptide chains nor haem groups can as yet be recognized.” The method worked, but what the picture lacked was depth.

In the spring of 1954, while Perutz was still comparing maps with and without mercury, Dorothy Hodgkin, née Crowfoot, stopped by the lab. "If you could get two heavy-atom derivatives," she told him, as he later recalled, "you could determine the structure in three dimensions." A derivative is the same protein crystallized in the same lattice with a heavy atom fixed at a definite site, and the mercury crystal was one such<Fn>Its two mercury atoms counted as one derivative, since they were added together at symmetry-related sites and gave a single comparison per spot</Fn>. For any reflection outside the symmetry axis set, a single comparison leaves two candidate phases, because there are two directions the protein wave can point so that adding the mercury’s wave gives the measured result. Hodgkin pointed to a passage at the end of [Bijvoet’s 1951 paper on strychnine](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X51000891), where the Dutch crystallographer proposed that a second derivative, with a heavy atom at a different site, could settle which of the two was right. Full phase angles for every reflection would make a three-dimensional map possible.

The path forward was set, and almost immediately Perutz hit a setback. When he grew the next batch of mercury crystals, the unit cell came out with one of its edges twice as long as before. The molecules were no longer stacking the way they did in the native crystal, which meant the two patterns could no longer be compared spot by spot. "This seemed most baffling and its cause quite obscure," Perutz said. "We thought it might be different horses, for example; it might be some impurities; all kinds of hypotheses. I got quite desperate about it."

Then, Perutz was hit with another setback. In the autumn of 1954, he fell seriously ill. By late summer 1955, unable to work, he thought he would have to resign. It was a colleague who then suggested celiac disease, a reaction to the gluten in wheat flour, and once he removed bread and flour from his diet, he began to recover, though only partly<Fn>Other foods turned out to affect him too, and for the rest of his life he stuck to a short diet of meat, fish and mild cheese, with a few vegetables.</Fn>.

While Perutz was ill and the doubled unit cell issue remained, Kendrew pushed forward with the mercury method on myoglobin, the protein he had been working on since 1948. It was a simpler protein, with one heme and one iron, a quarter of hemoglobin’s weight. Its job was to store oxygen in muscle. His [first maps of horse myoglobin](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1950.0044), in 1948, showed the same parallel rods, a one-chain “penny” to hemoglobin’s four stacked layers. Unlike Perutz with hemoglobin, however, Kendrew did not manage to grow great crystals. Horse myoglobin crystals never grew past fine needles, which were too small for detailed x-ray work, and neither did myoglobin from porpoises, seals, dolphins, penguins, tortoises or carp. It wasn’t until late 1952 that Kendrew found a source that gave “the most marvellous myoglobin crystals”, coming “of all places” from a stock of sperm whale left over from wartime trials<Fn>Further batches were shipped to him from Lima, Peru, where sperm whale meat was still sold in the market.</Fn>.

The mercury trick, however, would not work on myoglobin because it had no free cysteines for the mercury to bond to<Fn>Ingram tried some other routes, like using a compound that bonds to the heme iron, but those didn’t work either.</Fn>. Howard Dintzis, a biophysicist with heavy-atom expertise who had recently joined the team, began systematically trying to find suitable compounds. He set up racks of small bottles, each holding crystals of hemoglobin or myoglobin, and to each added a different compound. After a few weeks of soaking, he took x-ray photographs of the crystals and compared them with the native pattern. What he was looking for was the same thing that made mercury work for hemoglobin: identical unit cell dimensions, the spots in the same places but at different intensities. Several myoglobin compounds passed this test. By the middle of 1955, they had myoglobin crystals with heavy atoms at five different places on the molecule's surface, each crystal packed the same way as the native ones. With myoglobin giving results, hemoglobin stuck on the doubled unit cell, and Perutz working part-time while he recovered, the hemoglobin bottles got little attention, and several hundred of them went unexamined.

Kendrew computed a projection of the molecule, and like Perutz’s, it showed nothing he could make sense of. So, he began preparing for a three-dimensional map, which needed the position of each heavy atom in all three dimensions. The down-the-axis photographs could not give him that. They recorded the molecules as if squashed flat along the crystal’s symmetry axis, the same view as the projection, so they showed where every heavy atom sat across the plane but not how deep it was. Without the depths, the phases could not be calculated.

Kendrew could not find where each heavy atom sat along the symmetry axis because the crystal had no fixed point along it to measure from. If every atom in it moved the same distance along the axis, every spot on every photograph would stay exactly as it was, so the photographs could not tell one position from another. What they could record was how far apart two heavy atoms sat along the axis. Kendrew found that distance by comparing the photographs of two derivatives with each other, instead of each with the native protein<Fn>Several people worked on this problem at once. Perutz combined suggestions from Crick and William Cochran into a method, but fell ill before publishing it: "This method was just waiting in my notebook," he said. David Harker in Brooklyn and Bragg each proposed methods of their own. Kendrew drew on these and fixed the final positions with a calculation run on the computer.</Fn>. With the distances between the five heavy atoms known, he could compute the phases.

Kendrew said later that from this point the solution of myoglobin was inevitable. He planned to work first at a resolution of six angstroms, which he expected would be enough to separate neighboring stretches of the polypeptide chain but not enough to show the side chains or individual atoms. At that resolution only the innermost spots on the photograph were needed. "It was only three hundred reflections, you see," he said. "Three hundred spots. From each of three or four different heavy-atom derivatives." By his estimate, the whole of the data collection and the calculation for that first low-resolution map could have been done in about two weeks.

For measuring intensities, he replaced the comparison of spots against a chart by eye with a densitometer<Fn>Kendrew had seen the densitometer used on tissue sections by a colleague in London and traveled there to use it after hours until the unit could buy its own.</Fn>. The three-dimensional Fourier synthesis, which meant summing hundreds of waves for each point in the unit cell, went to the EDSAC, the digital computer at the Mathematical Laboratory<Fn>Kendrew’s first research student, Hugh Huxley, complained in 1949 about a two-week hand calculation to his friend John Bennett in Maurice Wilkes’s EDSAC group. Bennett then wrote a program that did it in half an hour. Kendrew learned to program with Bennett’s help and the two published [the first paper on crystallographic computing](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X52000228) with a digital computer in 1952. Perutz, on the other hand, did not trust the machine and kept his calculations on Hollerith punched cards.</Fn>. What could in theory have been done in two weeks took two years.

In 1956, while Kendrew was pushing ahead with myoglobin, Perutz was still working part-time. Hemoglobin remained stuck on the doubled unit cell, and additional heavy-atom derivatives hadn’t been found either. The compounds that worked for myoglobin did not work for hemoglobin, echoing the issues that Kendrew ran into when he first tried the mercury trick. Hemoglobin “didn’t seem to have [the] niches” where the compounds got lodged like sperm whale myoglobin did. It seemed that the reactive cysteines that mercury attached to were the only “hooks” available for hemoglobin, and those were already taken. Dintzis’s workaround was to block hemoglobin’s reactive cysteines with iodoacetamide, a small reagent, so that when the crystals were soaked in a mercury compound again, the mercury would be forced to bind somewhere else, if it could. He set these up, along with hundreds of other preparations, before returning to the United States in 1956.

Dintzis did not look at them. After he left, over the winter of 1956-57, Ann Cullis, Perutz’s research assistant, found the preparations still on the shelves and they began going through them one by one. Most of the bottles failed, but one of the blocked-hemoglobin bottles, soaked in mercuridiacetate, passed. The unit cell matched the native one and its intensity changes were different from the previous mercury-attached hemoglobin, which meant that the mercury was sitting in a different position. Now, hemoglobin had a heavy atom in a second position, and the two-derivative method that Hodgkin had suggested three years earlier could be tried.

That spring of 1957, Perutz went back to working full days, and the solution to the doubled cell mystery came quickly. “A chance observation gave me the idea that the effect might have something to do with the acidity of the solution,” he told Judson later. “Once I had the idea, I of course solved it in an afternoon.” Hemoglobin could pack into two different crystal lattices, and the switch between them was reversible and depended on pH. The transition point was just about the degree of acidity that Perutz grew his crystals at, which was why the mercury crystals had come out with one edge doubled. With a little acid or alkali, the doubling disappeared. In the space of a few months, the two problems that had held back hemoglobin since 1954 were both gone.

In the summer of 1957, Kendrew’s two years of work came through. He got his electron density map of sperm whale myoglobin at a resolution of six angstroms, the first protein structure ever solved. There were sixteen contour maps, each a slice through the unit cell, just under two angstroms apart. Kendrew had each one traced onto a sheet of clear plastic, and the sheets bolted together into a block a foot long and eight inches thick. From any side of the block, the electron density could now be seen in three dimensions. From this, he built a [plasticine model](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co13543/kendrews-original-model-of-the-myoglobin-molecule-molecular-models-proteins). It “reminded many people of many things”, he later commented. A visitor to the MRC writing an internal report on the Cambridge unit said its most common association was "abdominal viscera"; the Italian crystallographer Alfonso Liquori called it “a horrible object, but beautiful work”; William Astbury, of keratin fame, called it “worm-like”; and a correspondent said it looked like “a dog quietly huddled up.” Kendrew was disappointed that it was not as pretty as the DNA double helix that Crick and James Watson had solved next door a few years earlier.

Pretty or not, elements of the molecule were now recognizable. One was the heme, a flat disk of electron density held to at least four neighboring stretches of chain with one face left open to oxygen. The other was the chain itself. At the six angstrom resolution, a tightly packed helical segment looks like a solid rod, and the straight runs all fitted Pauling’s alpha helix, even though it could not be proved at this resolution. “The big surprise was that it was so irregular,” Kendrew said later. The chain turned through large angles and wandered with no symmetry and no repeating geometry. When Kendrew and his colleagues [published](https://www.nature.com/articles/181662a0) their 6 Å map in Nature in March 1958, they wrote that the arrangement was “more complicated than has been predicated by any theory of protein structure.” Crick’s warnings from 1951 were confirmed. When Sanger [finished the insulin sequence](https://doi.org/10.1042/bj0600541) in 1955, he showed that its amino acids followed no simple repeating pattern. Now, Kendrew saw that the fold followed no simple repeating geometry either.

To get the three-dimensional map for hemoglobin, Perutz would have to follow more or less the same steps that Kendrew did, but on a larger scale. Hemoglobin had five thousand atoms, four times as many as myoglobin. Kendrew had needed three to four hundred spots for his map at 6 Å; Perutz needed more than a thousand for his 5.5 Å one, each photographed and measured in the native crystal and again in every derivative. Hemoglobin was also a less willing host for heavy atoms, so it took two years to reach the six derivatives a molecule this size needed. The intensities were read on the densitometer that Kendrew had introduced, and the Fourier summation ran on the EDSAC 2, one of the fastest computers then built.

At the end of August 1959, Perutz got his map. Like Kendrew, he traced his sections on plastic sheets, but where Kendrew had drawn the contours on his and bolted them into a stack to look through, Perutz cut the dense regions out of each sheet and stacked the pieces into a solid model, painting two chains black and two white, with a red disk for each heme. Twenty-two years after Haurowitz had told him to work out the structure of hemoglobin, Perutz finally had a shape. The molecule had four chains in two identical halves. Each chain was folded almost exactly as myoglobin’s was, and one half sat on top of the other, flipped upside down about the axis that Bernal and Perutz had found in the crystal symmetry two decades earlier. The four hemes sat in separate pockets at the surface, as far apart as they could be. That, Perutz said later, "was perhaps the greatest surprise the structure presented to us." Since the hemes were known to raise each other’s appetite for oxygen, it was assumed they would be close together.

At the same time Perutz got his 5.5 Å map, Kendrew pushed myoglobin from 6 Å to 2 Å. The 6 Å map had used only the innermost few hundred spots from each crystal; at 2 Å, across all the derivatives and repeat exposures, about 250,000 spots had to be photographed, measured, and corrected. Kendrew brought in new machines to measure them, and the calculation only just fit in the memory of the EDSAC 2.

At 2 Å the chain could be followed residue by residue and the side chains showed as distinct bumps, though the individual atoms were still a blur. The straight runs that had looked like the alpha helix in 1957 were now proved to be, and many of the bumps could be identified as particular amino acids<Fn>The map helped confirm or correct the sequencing of myoglobin that was underway at the Rockefeller</Fn>. The heme was now sharply visible and, against Kendrew's and Perutz's expectation, its iron atom sat more than a quarter of an angstrom out of the plane of the ring, attached to a helical segment by a histidine side chain<Fn>Pauling had proposed the same link on chemical grounds thirty years earlier</Fn>.

---

Twenty-six years after Bernal had wandered the streets of Cambridge wondering how much the first protein photographs might one day reveal, the first answers appeared in print. On 13 February 1960, Perutz’s [5.5 Å hemoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185416a0) and Kendrew’s [2 Å myoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185422a0) were published back-to-back in the same issue of Nature. In December 1962, Perutz and Kendrew received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their discoveries, the same year that Crick, Watson, and Maurice Wilkins received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering the structure of DNA. Bernal wrote to congratulate Perutz: what he and Kendrew had done was “only the first swallow but what a summer it is going to be.”<Fn>Quoted in Georgina Ferry, *Max Perutz and the Secret of Life* (2007), p. 190</Fn>

And indeed it was. Soon after, their method of isomorphous replacement enabled many others to determine the structure of other proteins. In 1965, David Phillips [solved lysozyme](https://doi.org/10.1038/206757a0) at the Royal Institution, the first enzyme structure. In 1967, one of Perutz's former students, David Blow, [solved chymotrypsin](https://doi.org/10.1038/214652a0), the enzyme whose twinned crystals Perutz had set aside thirty years earlier. Dorothy Hodgkin [solved insulin](https://doi.org/10.1038/224491a0) in 1969, thirty-four years after her first photograph of it. For the next three decades, isomorphous replacement remained the standard way to find phases.

Perutz kept working on hemoglobin and learning more of its secrets. In 1968 he published the structure of [horse oxyhemoglobin at 2.8 Å](https://www.nature.com/articles/219029a0), and then the [deoxy version](https://www.nature.com/articles/228551a0) followed in 1970. That same year, in a Nature [paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/228726a0), he explained how hemoglobin binds oxygen cooperatively, the concept Adair had described to him in 1937. By comparing the oxy and deoxy structures, he showed that when oxygen binds, the iron moves into the plane of the heme and pulls the histidine along with it, which tugs at the rest of the helix and shifts the subunits against each other, rotating one half of the molecule by about fifteen degrees against the other. He continued to work on hemoglobin until his death in 2002.

In 2020, when Andrei Lupas was stuck with his Af1503 structure problem, what he was actually stuck on was the phase problem. By then, most new structures were solved without isomorphous replacement. The usual shortcut was to take the solved structure of a related protein, calculate the phases it would give, and use them as a starting point with the measurements from the new crystal. This method, molecular replacement, had been [introduced in 1962](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X62000067) by two members of Perutz’s group, Michael Rossmann and David Blow. For Af1503, however, [no available structure was close enough](https://portlandpress.com/biochemj/article/478/10/1885/228727/The-breakthrough-in-protein-structure-prediction) to give usable phases, so molecular replacement had not worked.

AlphaFold2 helped by supplying a predicted model of Af1503 itself, close enough to the real one to work. Lupas’s group ran the molecular replacement with it, and the phases it gave were good enough to create a picture of the molecule. AlphaFold2 had produced that model by learning from the PDB, an archive that exists because Perutz, Kendrew, and many, many others at Cambridge and elsewhere spent a little over two decades painstakingly finding a way to turn spots on a film into three-dimensional pictures of molecules.
