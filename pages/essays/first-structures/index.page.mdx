Today, a biologist can find the shape of almost any protein in seconds. For several years now, AI tools have been able to also predict the shape from its sequence with high accuracy. [AlphaFold’s database](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) holds more than 200 million of these predicted structures, and other AI tools let researchers predict how drug molecules bind to proteins, judge which mutations are likely to cause disease, and design new proteins from scratch. All these tools were trained, directly or indirectly, on a far smaller archive: the Protein Data Bank (PDB), which holds a little over 250,000 structures, each measured in a lab, often after years of work on a single protein.

When the PDB opened in 1971, it included [just seven structures](https://www.rcsb.org/pages/about-us/history). Among the earliest were myoglobin and hemoglobin, solved in Cambridge in 1957 and 1959 by John Kendrew and Max Perutz, the first protein structures ever determined. [About 80%](https://rcsb.org/statistics/growth/experimental-method) of the quarter million structures measured since were solved by x-ray crystallography, tracing back to the method Perutz’s group worked out.

In 1937, when Perutz took his first photographs of hemoglobin crystals, he had his diffraction data as spots on film, but no way to turn them into a picture of the molecule. No one was sure whether the structure of a protein could be measured at all. And certainly, he didn’t expect it to take sixteen years to find the method, and twenty-two to see the molecule.

Along the way, as Perutz was trying to solve hemoglobin’s structure, there came war, exile, years of misleading results, and a younger colleague who told him that the model he was so proud of was wrong, until a letter from a stranger showed him the way. This is the story of those twenty-two years.

### X-Rays

Max Perutz joined J.D. Bernal’s lab in Cambridge by chance. He had asked a chemistry professor who was traveling there to put in a word for him at the biochemistry lab, but the professor came back with a place in x-ray crystallography instead. When Perutz protested that he knew nothing of crystallography, the professor told him, “You’ll learn it, my boy.”

Bernal had just begun moving x-ray crystallography from graphite and bronze into the realm of biology. In 1934, he and his research student, Dorothy Crowfoot, [produced](https://www.nature.com/articles/133794b0) the first x-ray diffraction photograph of a protein crystal, pepsin, overturning the widely held belief that proteins would not diffract at all. Crowfoot later recalled that on the night of the first photographs, Bernal “wandered about the streets of Cambridge, thinking of the future and of how much it might be possible to know about the structure of proteins if the photographs… could be interpreted in every detail.”

When Perutz arrived at Bernal’s lab, a small, dingy sub-department in the Cavendish, Bernal had no protein crystals left for him. He set him to work on minerals from blast-furnace slag instead. “Nasty minerals” were not what Perutz had come for, but he used them to learn x-ray crystallography.

The technique works by shining a narrow beam of x-rays through a crystal and recording, on photographic film placed behind it, the pattern of spots the crystal scatters them into, an effect [discovered](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19133461004) in 1912. In principle, from where the spots fall and how dark they are, one can work back to where the atoms sit inside the crystal. By the time Perutz arrived in Cambridge, the method had been used to determine the structures of minerals, metals, and organic molecules of a few dozen atoms — but certainly nothing as large as a protein.

Spots form because x-rays are waves, and waves can combine. The x-rays used in crystallography have wavelengths of about a tenth of a nanometer, or one ångström (Å), which is roughly the distance between neighboring atoms in a molecule. Like other waves, they have amplitude (height), which sets how bright they are, and a phase, which describes where along its cycle a wave is, or where its crests fall. When waves are added, those in step reinforce each other, and those opposed cancel each other out.

When x-rays strike a molecule, its electrons send out new waves of their own, in every direction. A single molecule scatters far too little to record. But in a crystal, trillions of identical copies are stacked like bricks in every direction. Familiar crystals like salt, sugar and quartz are built this way, and under the right conditions, proteins can form crystals too. Each brick, in this instance — the small box holding one or a few molecules — is called the “<Term k="unitCell">unit cell</Term>.”

The spots on the film record two kinds of information. The pattern they create comes from the spacing *between* the unit cells. The waves from all the copies line up only in certain directions; wherever they do, they make a spot, and everywhere else they cancel. How dark the spots are depends on the arrangement of atoms *within* each unit cell. Because those atoms sit in different places, their waves combine differently in each direction: when they mostly reinforce each other, the spot is dark, and when they partly cancel, the spot is faint. Thus, the positions of the spots measure the unit cell, and the darkness of the spots is a coded picture of the molecule inside.

### The Big Idea

Fed up with blast-furnace slag, Perutz began thinking about using x-ray crystallography on parts of proteins. In the summer of 1937, while visiting Prague, he raised the idea with his cousin’s husband, a young professor named Felix Haurowitz. Perutz was considering hemin, a small salt found in hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in blood. But Haurowitz talked him out of it and told him to go for the *whole* hemoglobin instead. Haurowitz even suggested a physiologist in Cambridge, Gilbert Adair, who could provide hemoglobin crystals.

When Perutz got back to Cambridge, he approached Adair, who grew him crystals of horse methemoglobin — a stable, oxidized form of hemoglobin — apologizing that they were “probably much too small” as he handed them over. They turned out to be excellent for x-ray work<Fn>Perutz had also been given crystals of chymotrypsin, a digestive enzyme sent from the Rockefeller Institute, but they were twinned — two lattices grown through one another — so there was no telling which spots belonged to which. Adair’s hemoglobin gave clean single crystals, and so it became, by elimination, Perutz’s sole focus.</Fn>.

That winter of 1937–38, Perutz got his first encouraging results. The photographs of Adair’s wet crystals gave sharp spots right up to the edge of the film. This meant that every hemoglobin molecule in the crystal was identical to every other, down to the positions of their atoms. From the spacing of the spots near the center of the film, he measured the unit cell, and from the symmetry of the pattern, he showed that each molecule was made of two identical halves.

Perutz published these results in [a letter to *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/141523a0) on 19 March 1938<Fn>Written with Bernal and Isidor Fankuchen, an American crystallographer. From the unit cell’s volume and the crystal’s density, Perutz worked out that each cell held two molecules, each weighing about 69,000 times as much as a hydrogen atom, close to the figures Adair and Svedberg had measured by other methods.</Fn>, but he was still a long way from *solving* the structure. The issue, at the time, was that the film recorded only part of the information needed to reconstruct the molecule. Each spot’s darkness told Perutz how strong a scattered x-ray wave was, but it did not reveal the wave’s phase — where its crests and troughs lined up relative to those of other waves. The phases are critical because two waves of equal strength can produce completely different results when added together. Aligned crest to crest, they reinforce one another; aligned crest to trough, they cancel.

To work backward from the spots to a picture of the molecule, then, Perutz needed to combine the waves mathematically. He knew their strengths, but without their phases, he did not know how to line them up. For smaller molecules, crystallographers could guess an arrangement of atoms, calculate the spots it would produce, and compare them with the film. For a molecule with thousands of atoms, there were far too many arrangements to try. Finding the missing phases was the “phase problem,” and it held up the structure of hemoglobin for the next fifteen years.

The week before the *Nature* letter appeared, the world changed. On 12 March, Hitler annexed Austria. German troops marched into Vienna, and Perutz’s parents, Jewish by descent, fled to Prague. In an instant, Max Perutz had become a refugee<Fn>To make matters worse, his parents would no longer be able to fund his Cambridge education. Amid this turmoil, however, Perutz still found a way to spend the summer in the mountains. A skier and mountaineer since childhood, he joined an expedition to the Swiss Alps to study how snow turns into glacier ice. The work, published the following year, gave him a reputation as a glacier expert that would come in use later.</Fn>.

At Cambridge too, more change awaited Perutz. That autumn, Lawrence Bragg, then the leading figure in x-ray crystallography, had just been appointed head of the Cavendish, and Perutz’s supervisor, Bernal, had moved to Birkbeck College in London, “taking all of biological crystallography with him except Perutz”<Fn>Bernal had been blocked from the Cambridge chair partly by his membership of the Communist Party.</Fn>. Perutz was nervous to show his new boss his work, but when he finally mustered the courage weeks later, he won Bragg over immediately. Perutz had taken “the best photographs of a protein at that time,” Bragg said, and they had “fired” his enthusiasm. Without delay, Bragg secured a grant from the Rockefeller Foundation to support Perutz’s research<Fn>Bragg’s words come from an interview with Horace Freeland Judson for *The Eighth Day of Creation*. The grant gave Perutz a paid position, which let him support his parents and bring them to England; they had fled from Prague to Switzerland after Czechoslovakia came under German pressure.</Fn>.

In January 1939, Bernal gave [a lecture](https://doi.org/10.1038/143663a0) on the phase problem. Direct analysis of the photographs, he said, “is rendered impossible by the fact that we can never know the phases of the reflections corresponding to the different spots.” He speculated on two ways to solve this problem. First, perhaps it would be possible to recover some of the missing phase information by watching how the diffraction pattern changed as the crystal dried. Protein crystals contain water between their molecules. As that water leaves, the crystal can shrink, bringing the molecules closer together and changing how their scattered waves reinforce or cancel one another. If the molecules themselves kept their shape, comparing photographs at different stages of drying might help narrow down which phases could account for the changing intensities.

The second idea, which Bernal only briefly mentioned, was to add a heavy atom to the molecule<Fn>The idea was not new. Three years earlier, J. Monteath Robertson had worked out the complete structure of phthalocyanine, a blue dye of fifty-eight atoms, by comparing two crystals of it, one with a nickel atom at the center of the molecule and one without. The nickel changed how dark the spots were, and from those changes Robertson could work out where every atom sat.</Fn>. Such an atom, with its many electrons, scatters x-rays strongly. If its position in the crystal was known, then the waves it contributed could be calculated, including their phases, and used as a reference to read the protein’s wave.

Both ideas, in essence, meant changing the crystal in a controlled way and comparing photographs to see whether each spot got darker or fainter. Under the right conditions, those changes might reveal the phases.

Perutz decided to try the first path. But in September 1939, Britain declared war on Germany, and in May 1940, just a few weeks after he received his PhD, Perutz was arrested when the Home Office extended internment to men previously judged harmless. He was shipped to a camp of wooden huts above Quebec City, where the internees slept, a hundred men to a room, double-bunked, behind barbed-wire windows. His colleagues campaigned for his release, and in January 1941 he landed at Liverpool and was told to register with the Cambridge police as an [enemy alien](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1985/08/12/enemy-alien)<Fn>When war broke out, tribunals had sorted German and Austrian nationals into security risks, doubtful cases and refugees from Nazi oppression, and Perutz had been placed in the last group, free to carry on his work. He told the whole story of his internment in “Enemy Alien,” *The New Yorker*, 12 August 1985.</Fn>.

### The Phase Problem

Back at Cambridge, Perutz returned to drying crystals. Rapid drying destroyed the fine detail in the diffraction patterns, so he set out to find a way to slow the process. He sealed each crystal in a thin glass capillary with a trace of its own mother liquor — the salt solution in which it had grown — at each end. This way, it could dry over several days and be held at each stage for weeks.

Perutz photographed the crystal at four stages and found that the shrinkage was lopsided. The crystal contracted almost entirely in one direction, so he concluded that the molecules packed in layers that closed up as the water between them drained away. He [published these results](https://doi.org/10.1038/149491a0) in *Nature* in May 1942. From the same photographs, he concluded that each molecule was flat, “a platelet 36 Å thick”, an interpretation that began a wrong turn that would take more than a decade to correct.

Drying, however, changed two things at once. As the water drained, the layers moved closer and the solution between them grew saltier, and salt, with its heavier atoms, scatters x-rays more strongly, so Perutz could not tell which change had made a spot darker or fainter. In 1942, a new crop of crystals, prepared in pure water, gave him a way around that. Their spots fell in the same places as before, showing the molecules were packed identically, but many were darker or fainter, because pure water scatters more weakly than salty liquid. By soaking salt back in a little at a time, Perutz could make the liquid scatter more strongly while leaving the protein alone.

Each spot now combined two waves: the protein’s, which Perutz wanted to know, and the liquid’s, which he could change. When he compared photographs, a spot that darkened as salt went in meant the two waves were in step; one that faded meant they were opposed.

Not every spot would do, though. To read the protein’s wave, Perutz had to know the liquid’s, and that was possible only for one column of spots through the center of the film. Those spots saw the crystal straight down through its layers, like a core cut through a layer cake, and in that view the liquid was simply a flat layer of known thickness and saltiness.

These spots had a second advantage. Because each molecule had two identical halves, every layer looked the same flipped upside down, so each protein wave had to have a crest or a trough exactly at the layer’s middle: in step with the liquid’s or opposed, plus or minus. For the seven spots nearest the center, the salt test told him which<Fn>Two of the seven signs actually came from the drying photographs: as the crystal shrank, a row of spots faded to nothing and came back wherever a sign changed, so the signs could be read off in order. Bragg described these “nodes and loops” in “First stages in the x-ray analysis of proteins,” *Reports on Progress in Physics* 28 (1965), pp. 8–9.</Fn>.

Perutz could finally add the seven waves together. Since they looked straight down through the layers, they gave only a single line: how much matter lay at each depth through a protein layer. It had four bumps about nine ångströms apart, which he read as four thin sheets of protein chain stacked on one another.

He and a research assistant [published the result](https://doi.org/10.1038/151714a0) in *Nature* in June 1943<Fn>The research assistant was Joy Boyes-Watson. The same note printed a rough two-dimensional map from an air-dried crystal, built from only four spots, which showed dumb-bell-shaped molecules. “Nevertheless, with all its shortcomings,” they wrote, “it is the first direct picture of a small protein molecule.”</Fn>. After six years, the first look inside the molecule was a line graph with four bumps — one-dimensional data for a three-dimensional structure. The method worked, but the four sheets were a guess, and they would turn out to be wrong.

</Wrap>

In 1943, Perutz was sent as a glacier expert to an Allied wartime project<Fn>The project, Habakkuk, was to build an aircraft carrier out of pykrete, ice mixed with wood pulp, an idea by the inventor Geoffrey Pyke. Perutz tested it in a refrigerated meat store beneath London’s Smithfield Market, hidden behind frozen carcasses, and found that it slowly sagged unless kept below about −16 °C. Keeping a ship that cold would have taken so much steel that, as Perutz put it, the U.S. Navy decided “Habakkuk was a false prophet.”</Fn>. When he returned in 1944, he found that his two research assistants had spent his absence photographing hemoglobin crystals and grading thousands of spots by eye<Fn>The assistants were Joy Boyes-Watson and Edna Davidson. With no instrument to read darkness, they graded each spot against a calibration strip, a row of about twenty test exposures running from barely visible to nearly black. Recording a single layer of spots took eighteen photographs, and their estimates could be off by 20–25% for strong spots and 30–40% for weak ones.</Fn>. A picture of the molecule would need spots in every direction analyzed — not just one column — but without their phases, there was no way to add their waves together into a map.

There was a different kind of map, however, that didn’t need phases. If he added the waves using only their heights, as though every wave had its crests at the same starting point, the result would not be a map of the atoms but a map of the distances *between* them, called a Patterson map. Since it needed only the intensities of spots, Perutz could make one from his photographs alone.

To understand how a Patterson map works, picture a dark classroom. You can’t see where the children are sitting, but you have a pack of notes, each telling you how far apart one pair is, and in what direction. For example, it might say “Sam is 1 step to the right of Ali,” or “Brian is 2 steps in front of Mia.” Now suppose you go to the whiteboard, mark a dot in the middle, and for each note, you walk the steps it describes from that dot and put a sticker where you land. If the children’s desks are laid out in neat rows, then many pairs would be “1 step to the right” or “2 steps forward,” and many notes would send you to the same places and the stickers pile up. If, however, the children are scattered at random, the stickers land all over the board and no pattern stands out.

A Patterson map is built the same way: each pair of atoms adds a peak, and the more pairs share a spacing, the taller the peak grows. Perutz’s first maps, completed in 1947, used the spots from only one view of the crystal, so they were flat, the whole molecule squashed onto one plane. Millions of pairs piled into a tangle, and all that stood out was the molecule’s outside: a squat cylinder, 57 Å across and 34 Å high, “the fuzzy outline of the molecule”<Fn>Boyes-Watson, Davidson and Perutz, Proc. R. Soc. A 191 (1947).</Fn>. Ten years after his first photographs, the inside was still only the guess of four sheets.

Partway through those years, early in 1946, Perutz got a new partner. John Kendrew, a physical chemist, had spent part of the war in Southeast Asia, where he kept running into Bernal, who talked to him about proteins and Perutz’s work. Kendrew came back to Cambridge wanting to work on proteins himself, and Perutz taught him everything he knew about crystallography.

Perutz, meanwhile, had already set out to make the map in three dimensions, as a stack of slices, one for each depth, which he called the only way to make the whole diffraction pattern “at least potentially capable of being interpreted.” But the map would be readable only if the inside was orderly. Chains running side by side in bundles or flat layers would repeat the same spacings thousands of times and pile up into straight rods, while an irregular structure would leave a featureless, unintelligible blur. Every plausible model of a protein like hemoglobin assumed the orderly kind. Perutz too expected rods, as further evidence of his stacked sheets.

Measuring the 7,000 or so spots the map needed was a task “whose length and tediousness it will be better not to describe,” Perutz wrote<Fn>Adding up the waves was beyond any machine in Cambridge, so the sums went to the Scientific Computing Service in London, which ran them on punched-card machines using a procedure Dorothy Crowfoot’s group in Oxford had devised for penicillin. In all, the calculation took some 400 million additions.</Fn>. By the spring of 1948, the calculation was done: 31 contour maps, one for each slice through the unit cell, which Perutz traced onto transparent sheets and stacked to see the peaks in three dimensions.

The rods were there. Several peaks stretched into long, straight ridges, all running the same way: one through the center of the map and six surrounding it. Perutz read them as protein chains lying side by side in four layers, matching the four sheets he had proposed in 1943.

On 8 June 1948, Perutz [submitted his findings](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1949.0005) to the Royal Society. The “parallel rods” structure, he wrote, was “plausible and consistent with all the known evidence, but proof for the time being” eluded him, and he ended the paper by wondering whether hemoglobin could be taken any further at all.

### A Glimmer of Hope

That same month, Perutz and Kendrew<Fn>Kendrew’s thesis came from the physiologist Joseph Barcroft, who suggested he look for the structural reason fetal hemoglobin behaves differently from adult hemoglobin, and supplied fetal hemoglobin from sheep. By 1948 Kendrew had moved on to myoglobin.</Fn> presented their work at a symposium on hemoglobin and wrote [a review](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.6422) of what x-ray crystallography could and could not do for large molecules. In it, they called the phase problem “the fundamental difficulty of all X-ray analysis” and described the two tricks crystallographers had used to get around it for smaller molecules.

One was the heavy-atom method, which involved attaching to the molecule an atom so heavy that its wave outweighs those of all the light atoms around it, so that each spot takes on roughly the heavy atom’s phase. Once that atom’s position is located, its phases can be used. The other was isomorphous replacement, which involved comparing two crystals identical except for one swapped atom. Their spots fall in the same places, and since the swapped atom’s wave can be calculated, whether each spot darkens or fades shows whether the atom’s wave and the protein’s wave are in step or opposed — the same logic as Perutz’s salt experiment.

They then ruled out both for proteins because of their size: “even the heaviest of heavy atoms would make a negligible contribution to the reflexions from so large a unit cell, so it would exert no control over the phases.” A hemoglobin molecule has about 5,000 atoms, with tens of thousands of electrons, so adding a mercury atom’s 80 electrons was not expected to make a measurable difference<Fn>Perutz also knew that Dorothy Crowfoot had tried adding heavy atoms to insulin, a much smaller protein, and seen no detectable change.</Fn>.

With phases out of reach, Perutz and Kendrew stuck with Patterson maps. For nearly two years, they tried guessing chain shapes and comparing those maps to measured ones, but none stood out<Fn>In March 1950 they admitted that their models had “no obvious advantages over their alternatives” (Bragg, Kendrew and Perutz, Proc. R. Soc. A 203, 1950). They had ignored a chemical fact the American chemist Linus Pauling had established years earlier: the bonds joining amino acids cannot rotate freely. Pauling built that constraint into the model he [published](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.37.4.205) in 1951 as the alpha helix. Bragg, many years later, called the 1950 paper “the most ill-planned and abortive” piece of work he had been involved in (“First stages,” p. 6).</Fn>.

Meanwhile, in the autumn of 1949, a 33-year-old physicist named Francis Crick joined the Medical Research Council unit that Perutz and Kendrew ran at the Cavendish. He was new to biology, had never done crystallography, and learned it largely by arguing with the two of them. He began by checking the assumption, held since 1943, that hemoglobin was built from layers of parallel chains. If Perutz’s model were right, he calculated, the rods in the 1948 map should have been far taller than they were. He concluded that, since far fewer atom pairings shared the spacing their layered model required, the chains could not run straight and parallel for long; after about a dozen amino acids, they had to turn a corner.

In 1951, Crick presented these findings to Bragg, Perutz, and Kendrew in a talk titled *What Mad Pursuit*, essentially telling them that their assumptions for the last near-decade were incorrect. As Perutz would put it many years later: “Here was this new fellow… [who] was telling us that what we were so proud of, was wrong.”

With guesswork exhausted, by 1952 Bragg and Perutz [concluded](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1952.0136) that the only course left was a “direct attack” — better measurement. Using a Geiger counter, Perutz measured spots on an absolute scale: what share of the x-ray beam each spot caught instead of grading it by eye<Fn>The same measurements gave [the molecule’s outline](https://doi.org/10.1107/S0365110X52000721), a “rather modest” result, Bragg later wrote, but “we snatched at such small successes to keep ourselves in heart to carry on” (“First stages,” pp. 7, 9).</Fn>. But better measurement still could not give the phases, and everything inside the molecule stayed in the dark.

### Heavy Atoms

Early in 1953, a letter arrived at the Cavendish from Austen Riggs, a young biochemist at Harvard whom Perutz had never met. Riggs had read Perutz’s two short letters on sickle-cell hemoglobin and enclosed [a paper of his own](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.36.1.1)<Fn>In 1950 and 1951, Perutz had published two short letters on sickle-cell anemia, a disease in which red blood cells deform into a crescent, or sickle, shape after giving up their oxygen. Linus Pauling’s group had just traced it to a fault in the hemoglobin molecule itself.</Fn>. It was about the cooperation Adair had measured in the 1920s: the first of hemoglobin’s four hemes takes up oxygen reluctantly, and each of the others more readily, so a graph of oxygen taken up against oxygen pressure rises in an S-shape. Riggs wanted to know whether hemoglobin’s cysteines — amino acids that end in a sulphur atom — played a part in it. He capped them with mercury, which grips sulphur tightly, and the curve flattened but stayed S-shaped<Fn>Riggs had used human hemoglobin, which did not form crystals suited to x-ray work. Perutz’s horse hemoglobin also had two cysteines the mercury could reach.</Fn>.

“I jumped when I saw that,” Perutz said years later, “because it was clear to me that if it left the biological properties intact, then it would also leave the structure intact.” If the protein kept its shape, it would pack into crystals just as before, with the mercury at the same place in every unit cell. Two crystals identical except for a heavy atom were exactly what isomorphous replacement needed.

Five years earlier, Perutz and Kendrew had ruled the method out because one atom among 5,000 could not outweigh the rest. That was still true, but it only mattered for the heavy-atom method<Fn>A reminder of the difference: in the heavy-atom method, one atom is so heavy that its wave dominates every spot, so each spot’s phase is close to that atom’s own, which can be calculated once the atom is located. No atom is heavy enough to do that in a protein.</Fn>. Isomorphous replacement needed the mercury only to make the spots *measurably* darker or fainter. Perutz’s Geiger-counter measurements of 1951 had shown that in an average spot, the waves of a molecule’s tens of thousands of electrons mostly cancelled, adding up to the wave of only a few hundred. A mercury atom’s 80, scattering together from one point, would be a sizeable share of that. Riggs had shown that the first half of the experiment — putting a heavy atom on a protein without changing it — could be done.

Perutz tried the mercury on his own crystals, where it bound to two cysteines, one on each half of the molecule<Fn>The mercury derivative was prepared by Vernon Ingram, a chemist in the unit; David Green helped Perutz with the first projections.</Fn>. On the photographs, the spots fell in exactly the same places as before, but many were visibly darker or fainter. Perutz developed the two films in the summer of 1953 and, as he later told it, ran to fetch Bragg down to the darkroom to see them side by side. It was the first time a heavy atom had measurably changed the diffraction pattern of a protein.

</Wrap>

Over the following months, Perutz turned the two films into [signs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0203), using the same logic he had used with salt in 1943. Comparing the two films showed where the mercury sat, so its wave in every spot could be calculated, and whether a spot darkened or faded gave the sign of the protein’s wave. “Everything checked and double-checked perfectly,” Bragg wrote later; “it was a thrilling time.”

Bragg and Perutz summed the signed waves into an electron density map, which shows where the molecule’s electrons crowd, the first such map of a protein built from phases that had been measured rather than guessed. Because the signs came from a single layer of spots, the map was flat, like Perutz’s Patterson maps of the 1940s. [The paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1954.0205) reported that “neither polypeptide chains nor haem groups can as yet be recognized,” though it did settle one old question: there were “no regularly spaced layers,” and so no four sheets. Perutz called the image “entirely enigmatic”; Bragg likened it to “the last camp before the summit.”

The method worked, but the map lacked depth. For that, the rest of the spots were needed too, and for those, plus or minus was no longer enough — a protein wave’s crests could fall anywhere relative to the mercury’s<Fn>Plus or minus worked only for spots viewed straight along the axis relating the two identical halves: the 1943 column and the flat mercury maps. From that direction the molecule looked the same flipped over, which forced each wave to be in step with the reference wave or opposed to it. From any other angle, a wave’s crests could fall anywhere in its cycle.</Fn>. In the spring of 1954, while Perutz was puzzling over his flat maps, Dorothy Hodgkin, née Crowfoot, stopped by the lab. “If you could get two heavy-atom derivatives,” she told him, “you could determine the structure in three dimensions.” With one derivative, like mercury, the change in a spot’s darkness showed how far the protein’s crests lay from the mercury’s, but not whether they were ahead or behind<Fn>The mercury crystals counted as one derivative even though each molecule carried two mercury atoms, because the two sat at matching places on the two halves and gave a single comparison for each spot.</Fn>. A second, with a heavy atom in a different place, would add a second known wave, and only one possibility would fit both.

<Wrap>

The path forward needed a second derivative, but almost immediately Perutz hit roadblocks. The next batch of mercury crystals packed differently, so their films could no longer be compared spot by spot with the plain ones<Fn>The new crystals’ unit cell came out with one edge twice as long as before. Hemoglobin could pack into two different lattices depending on the acidity of the solution, and Perutz had been growing his crystals at almost exactly the point where it flipped. “Once I had the idea,” he said, “I of course solved it in an afternoon.”</Fn>. Then, in the autumn of 1954, he fell seriously ill, and as the illness stretched into late summer 1955, he thought he would have to resign, until a colleague suggested celiac disease and a diet without gluten brought him back part-time. “There was Kendrew going ahead splendidly,” he said later, “and I — after having produced the method — was getting absolutely nowhere!”

Since 1948, Kendrew had been working on myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen in muscle, and while Perutz was ill, he pushed ahead with isomorphous replacement. Myoglobin was a simpler protein, with a single heme and about a quarter of hemoglobin’s size, and Kendrew had found excellent crystals of it from sperm whale<Fn>Kendrew’s horse myoglobin crystals had never grown past fine needles, too small for detailed x-ray work. Sperm whale myoglobin gave far better ones, and further batches of the meat were shipped to him from Lima, Peru, where it was still sold in the market.</Fn>. The mercury trick, however, did not work on myoglobin, which had no free cysteines for the mercury to bond to. Howard Dintzis, a biophysicist who had recently joined the team, began systematically searching for alternatives. He set up racks of small bottles, each holding crystals of hemoglobin or myoglobin soaked in a different compound. Weeks later he photographed them, looking for what had made mercury work: spots in the same places, but darker or fainter. By the middle of 1955, myoglobin had five heavy-atom derivatives.

</Wrap>

Kendrew now had what Hodgkin had described: enough derivatives to pin down the phase of every spot, in every layer. Once he had worked out how deep each heavy atom sat, he could see that the solution of myoglobin was inevitable<Fn>The flat views showed where each heavy atom sat across the crystal, but not how deep, and the phases could not be calculated without that. Perutz, Bragg, and David Harker in Brooklyn each proposed ways round it, and Kendrew drew on them to fix the final positions.</Fn>. He planned to work first at a resolution of six ångströms — meaning that features closer together than that would blur — which was enough to separate neighboring stretches of the chain, but not to show individual atoms. Instead of grading spots by eye, his team measured them with a densitometer, and the waves were added up on the EDSAC, the university’s new electronic computer. By his estimate, the data collection and calculation for that first map could have been done in about two weeks. It took two years.

During those two years, several hundred of Dintzis’s hemoglobin bottles sat unexamined. Before returning to America in 1956, he had blocked hemoglobin’s two cysteines, the only places mercury ever bound, to force it to settle somewhere else. He never looked at the results. Over the winter, Perutz’s research assistant, Ann Cullis, went through the bottles one by one, and one showed “intensity differences of a kind we had not seen before,” as Perutz put it. The mercury had bound in a new place; hemoglobin had its second derivative. That spring, back at work full time, Perutz also traced the new packing to the acidity of the solution. In the space of a few months, the two problems that had held back hemoglobin since 1954 were both gone.

In the summer of 1957, Kendrew’s two years of photographing his derivatives, measuring their few hundred innermost spots, and running the sums on the EDSAC came through. He got his electron density map of sperm whale myoglobin at a resolution of six ångströms, the first protein structure ever solved. There were sixteen contour maps, each a slice through the unit cell, which he traced onto sheets of clear plastic and bolted together into a foot-long stack so he could look at the cloud of electrons from any angle. From it, he built a [plasticine model](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co13543/kendrews-original-model-of-the-myoglobin-molecule-molecular-models-proteins). He was disappointed with its ugliness. Next door, after Crick and James Watson had solved the structure of DNA, Watson said the double helix was so pretty it had to exist. Kendrew’s model, meanwhile, reminded people of “abdominal viscera.” One crystallographer summed it up succinctly: “a horrible object, but beautiful work.”<Fn>Both reactions are recorded in Soraya de Chadarevian, “John Kendrew and myoglobin: Protein structure determination in the 1950s,” *Protein Science 27* (2018): 1136–43. The second is from an unnamed fellow crystallographer reacting to the photograph in *Nature*.</Fn>

Pretty or not, parts of the molecule were now recognizable. One was the heme, a flat disk of electron density held to at least four neighboring stretches of chain, with one face left open to oxygen. The other was the chain itself. At six ångströms, a tightly packed helical segment looked like a solid rod, and the straight runs all fitted the alpha helix, a coil the American chemist Linus Pauling had proposed in 1951, though that could not yet be proved. “The big surprise was that it was so irregular,” Kendrew said later. Crick had been right in 1951 too: the chain ran straight for a stretch, then turned a corner, wandering through the molecule with no symmetry and no repeating pattern<Fn>Kendrew’s “big surprise” is from his interview with Judson (*The Eighth Day of Creation*, p. 539).</Fn>. When Kendrew and his colleagues [published](https://www.nature.com/articles/181662a0) their map in *Nature* in March 1958, they wrote that the arrangement was “more complicated than has been predicated by any theory of protein structure.”

For hemoglobin, Perutz followed the same path on a larger scale. It was four times the size of myoglobin and a less willing host for heavy atoms, and it took two more years to reach the six derivatives it needed. But by the end of August 1959, he got his map, at a resolution of five-and-a-half ångströms. It had taken more than a thousand spots, each measured in the plain crystals and again in all six derivatives, and the sums had run on EDSAC 2, one of the fastest computers then built.

Twenty-two years after Haurowitz had told him to work out the structure of hemoglobin, Perutz finally had a shape. He built a model from it, slice by slice, and for the first time could see the molecule whose spots he had been photographing since 1937. It had four chains, two of one kind and two of another, packed into a compact ball. The twofold axis he and Bernal had found in his first photographs ran through the center, making the two halves identical, each holding one chain of each kind. Every chain was folded almost the same way as Kendrew’s myoglobin; somehow, a protein from a horse’s blood had the shape of one from a whale’s muscle. And the four hemes sat in separate pockets on the surface, far apart. That, Perutz [said](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/lecture/), “was perhaps the greatest surprise the structure presented to us.” Since the hemes worked together to take up oxygen, it had been assumed that they would sit close to one another.

At the same time, Kendrew pushed myoglobin from six ångströms to two. That meant measuring the faint spots far beyond the inner circle, about a quarter of a million across all his crystals. The calculation only just fit in the memory of EDSAC 2. At two ångströms, the chain could be followed one amino acid at a time. The straight runs that had looked like Pauling’s alpha helix in 1957 were now proved to be exactly that, and the heme stood out sharply, its iron atom held to the chain by a single amino acid, a histidine<Fn>Kendrew called this “a striking confirmation” of suggestions made as much as thirty years earlier. James Conant had proposed in 1933 that the iron was bonded to the protein, and Jeffries Wyman, then Charles Coryell and Linus Pauling, argued that the bond was to a histidine</Fn>.

### Success

On 13 February 1960, Perutz’s [5.5 Å hemoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185416a0) and Kendrew’s [2 Å myoglobin structure](https://www.nature.com/articles/185422a0) were published back to back in *Nature*, twenty-six years after Bernal had wandered the streets of Cambridge wondering how much the first protein photographs might one day reveal. In December 1962, Perutz and Kendrew received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Bernal wrote to congratulate Perutz: what he and Kendrew had done was “only the first swallow but what a summer it [was] going to be”<Fn>Quoted in Georgina Ferry, *Max Perutz and the Secret of Life* (2007), p. 190.</Fn>.

Soon after, their method of isomorphous replacement enabled many others to determine the structures of other proteins. The first enzyme structure, that of [lysozyme](https://doi.org/10.1038/206757a0), was solved in 1965; the structure of [chymotrypsin](https://doi.org/10.1038/214652a0) was solved in 1967; and Dorothy Hodgkin solved the [structure of insulin](https://doi.org/10.1038/224491a0) in 1969, thirty-four years after taking her first x-ray photograph of it. For the next three decades, isomorphous replacement remained the standard way to find phases.

Perutz kept working on hemoglobin. In 1968, he published the structure of [horse oxyhemoglobin at 2.8 Å](https://www.nature.com/articles/219029a0), and by 1970 he had a map of hemoglobin without oxygen at the same detail. His aim, as he had put it at the [Nobel banquet](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1962/perutz/speech/), was to “explain the physiology of breathing in terms of the architecture of the haemoglobin molecule,” which his 1959 map had left unexplained.

Comparing those two, Perutz finally [saw](https://doi.org/10.1038/228726a0) how the molecule worked. Without oxygen, each iron sat slightly above the plane of its heme. When oxygen bound, the iron moved into the plane, and since it was bonded to its histidine, it pulled the histidine and its helix along. “When it clicks in and out of place it takes something with it,” he said. Without oxygen, a few weak links held the molecule’s two halves in a form that gripped oxygen loosely. Each oxygen that bound broke some of those links, and once enough had broken, the molecule switched to a form that took up oxygen readily. That was the cooperation Adair had measured a decade before handing Perutz his first crystals. The hemes acted on one another through the protein, which was why they could sit so far apart.
