## Target and tests

**Body now: 6,129. First target: 5,500.** Niko's 5,000 comes after that, if it
comes at all. With about 150 words of additions (the 1959 moment, a few "what
held" lines, the intro opener), that means **cutting about 780 words in all.**

Every passage gets three tests, all from Niko's note:

1. **Mechanism:** does it say what they wanted to learn, what they changed, and
   why the change would tell them something?
2. **Story:** does it move the story forward? Failed efforts get a sentence or
   two, unless what they taught is needed later.
3. **Assumed knowledge:** does it lean on a term or idea (an axis, a phase, a
   column) that the reader hasn't been given in plain words?

## Five rules for the whole pass

1. **Teach once, then call back in the same words.** Each idea gets one plain
   explanation at the point the story first needs it. After that it comes back
   as a clause in the *same words*. Never explain it again.
2. **Setbacks fast, the score kept.** A failed effort gets its want, its result,
   and one line on what it left them with. Then move on. Every beat ends by
   saying plainly what they now had and what was still missing, so the reader
   always knows what to keep.
3. **The measurement held; the reading didn't.** At each wrong turn (the 1942
   flat molecule, the 1943 four sheets, the 1948 rods) one short sentence says
   which part was right. This is the reader note, and it is also what keeps the
   setbacks from reading as pure loss.
4. **Body, figure and footnote each do one job.** The body says what happened and
   why it would work. The figure shows how. The footnote says how we know, or
   keeps a human detail. A footnote that only gives the mechanism in more detail
   gets deleted, not moved: no dumping ground.
5. **Slow down for the 1959 moment.** It gets more words than it has now, its
   own paragraphs, and the section's last word.

## The 1959 moment

Now it is one 161-word paragraph that gives the shape, the fold, the hemes and
"unexplained" in quick succession, and then Kendrew's 2 Å paragraph takes the
section's last word away from it. Proposal, about +100 words:

1. **Move Kendrew's 2 Å paragraph before it.** That map came the same year, so
   the timeline holds with a "by then" or "that summer". The section can then
   end on Perutz's moment, and "unexplained" hands straight to Success. The
   histidine is still planted before the ending.
2. **Split the moment into three short paragraphs:**
   - **The map and the model (new).** The map came as stacked contour sections.
     To see it, the team rolled out sheets of a thermosetting plastic to the
     thickness of a section, cut out every region dense enough to be chain
     ("From this section, for example, 14 shapes would be cut"), stacked the
     pieces by height, set the hemes in place and baked the model. The dense
     clouds curved through the molecule "like the vapour trails of an
     aeroplane." *Source: Perutz et al., Nature 185, 416 (1960), p. 417; the
     cutting detail the handoff asked to verify is confirmed here.*
   - **What they saw.** Four chains, two of each kind, painted black and white.
     The 1938 twofold axis runs through the centre (callback). The surfaces of
     the white chains "exactly fit those of the black." Each chain is folded
     like Kendrew's myoglobin: for a fair comparison they computed myoglobin at
     the same 5.5 Å and built it the same way, side by side (Nobel lecture fig.
     9 is the picture of this). Perutz's confidence, in the 1960 paper: "the
     results really prove themselves", since no combination of errors could
     have produced four chains, two matching pairs, and a match to myoglobin.
   - **The surprise (as now, given room).** The four hemes in separate pockets
     on the surface, the iron atoms of neighbouring black and white chains 25 Å
     apart (1960, p. 418, a concrete number for "far apart"). "The greatest
     surprise." "Unexplained." End of section.
3. **Figures:** Nobel lecture fig. 9 (myoglobin beside the white and black
   units) after "What they saw"; the rebuilt `PerutzModel` after the surprise.
   **Its title says "human"; the 1959 model was horse**, so fix it.

These are beats and sourced facts for you to write from, not drafted prose.

  decision A visually).
- **Core interactive: why spots (added 26 Oct).** One idea the essay has to
  land: a single molecule would light the whole film with its own smooth,
  continuous pattern, far too faint to record. The crystal's regular spacing
  makes the copies cancel everywhere except a few directions, and pours the
  light into those, so the film shows the molecule's pattern only at the
  spots, like a mask of pinholes that also brightens what comes through.
  Positions come from the spacing; darkness from the molecule. Prototyped on
  the lab page (`/essays/first-structures/lab`): section 8 (`AtomWaves`,
  each atom's extra path to a spot on an upright film, and the waves adding)
  feeds section 9 (`CellTimesLattice`, one cell's pattern × how well the cells
  line up = the film, with a cells-in-a-row toggle). Home in the essay: the
  "two kinds of information" paragraph in X-Rays, beside or instead of the
  lower half of `CrystalRecords`. It also sets up drying: shrinking the
  crystal moves the pinholes along the same pattern (nodes and loops).

## Decisions for you

**A. How much of "plus or minus" goes in the body?** Niko flagged axes as
assuming too much.
- *(a, my pick)* The body says the result plainly: "for these spots, the
  protein's wave could only be in step with the liquid's or exactly opposed,
  plus or minus, a consequence of the molecule's two identical halves," with
  the reason in a footnote (the line through a layer is symmetric, so each wave
  must have a crest or a trough at its middle) and in the four-bumps toggle.
  About word-neutral.
- *(b)* The claude.ai plan's five-step version in the body (about +35 words).
  Clearer for the keen reader, but it is the axis talk Niko found hard.

**B. The flat Patterson maps (parked move 3): cut now?** I'd say yes. They are
a failed effort, and the outline they gave is a clause.

**C. Chain fitting 1949–50 out of the body, with Pauling planted at 1957?** I'd
say yes. It's the most cuttable failed effort, and Crick's paragraph already
says "guessing failed" by implication.
